## Opening (0:00)

**0:00** · MIKE WILLIAMS (testimony, DVIDS 91190, part 1, source 5:16–5:23, 5:31–5:43): “And during our phone conversation we heard a, a gas level being announced from Sperry Sun. … My wife actually heard the announcement through the telephone, through the speaker, and asked if I needed to get off the phone to go take care of it. And I told her no, it was just an indication that, uh, to make everyone aware of what the gas levels are.”

**0:20** · NARRATOR: That was Mike Williams, the chief electronics technician on the Deepwater Horizon, testifying three months later. The call he described was on the night of April twentieth, twenty ten.

**0:31** · NARRATOR: The rig was drilling in the Gulf of Mexico, about fifty miles off Louisiana. A hundred and twenty-six people were on board.

**0:40** · NARRATOR: Earlier that evening, the crew had tested whether the well was sealed.

**0:44** · NARRATOR: The readings contradicted each other. One line showed no flow. The drill pipe, open to the same well, showed fourteen hundred psi.

**0:54** · NARRATOR: Around eight o'clock, the test was declared a success.

**0:59** · NARRATOR: The presidential commission later concluded that the pressure could only have come from a leak into the well, and called the decision a key error.

**1:07** · NARRATOR: Williams told investigators why the gas announcement that night did not alarm him.

**1:13** · MIKE WILLIAMS (testimony, DVIDS 91190, part 1, source 5:44–5:54): “We had gotten them so frequently that I'd actually become somewhat immune to them. I, I'd get to the point where I didn't even hear them anymore, uh, especially with this well, because we were getting gas back continuously.”

**1:23** · NARRATOR: By his account, the call was around nine thirty. At about nine forty, mud and gas burst onto the rig floor. Minutes later, the rig exploded.

**1:35** · NARRATOR: Eleven men were killed.

**1:42** · NARRATOR: Investigators would spend years asking how the well had been judged safe, and why the blowout preventer on the seafloor did not stop it.

## Main film

### 1 · The rig and the well (1:55)

**1:56** · NARRATOR: The Deepwater Horizon was a floating drilling rig, owned by Transocean and hired by BP.

**2:02** · NARRATOR: Below it lay about five thousand feet of water. A pipe called the riser ran down to the seafloor, to the blowout preventer: a three-hundred-sixty-ton stack of valves and steel blades on top of the well. From there, the well went down another two and a half miles into the rock.

**2:20** · NARRATOR: The well was about thirty-eight days behind schedule and some fifty-eight million dollars over budget. In an email that April, a BP engineer called it a nightmare well.

**2:31** · NARRATOR: A hundred and twenty-six people from thirteen companies were aboard. BP's well site leaders were Bob Kaluza and Don Vidrine. On the drill floor that evening were Transocean's toolpusher Jason Anderson and driller Dewey Revette.

**2:46** · NARRATOR: Halliburton had cemented the bottom of the well just after midnight. The plan now was to seal it temporarily, and move the rig on.

### 2 · Three barriers (2:54)

**2:55** · NARRATOR: Three things stood between the oil and gas in the rock and the rig above: the heavy drilling mud that filled the well, the cement at the bottom, and the blowout preventer.

**3:05** · NARRATOR: To leave, the crew would replace the mud in the riser and the top of the well with much lighter seawater. After that, the cement at the bottom would have to hold back the reservoir on its own.

**3:17** · NARRATOR: That morning, BP had sent home a team that could have checked the cement. Its decision plan said the check was not needed. The commission later concluded that BP should have run it.

**3:28** · NARRATOR: There was a warning in the laboratory, too. Halliburton's own tests had shown the cement mix to be unstable. The commission found no sign that the last of those results was ever reported to BP.

### 3 · The test (3:41)

**3:41** · NARRATOR: The cement would be checked instead with a negative pressure test: lower the pressure inside the well, as if the rig had already gone. If the cement held, nothing would come back: no pressure, and no flow.

**3:54** · NARRATOR: There was no standard procedure for running or reading this test. And BP and Transocean had not formally trained their people to do it.

**4:03** · NARRATOR: Starting around five p.m., the crew bled the pressure off the drill pipe. It came back. After repeated attempts, it built up to fourteen hundred psi.

**4:14** · NARRATOR: They discussed the readings on the rig floor. According to statements from both well site leaders, Jason Anderson said the pressure came from a so-called bladder effect: heavy mud in the riser pressing down on a closed preventer, and pushing pressure into the drill pipe.

**4:29** · NARRATOR: Anderson died that night and never gave his own account. A federal court later found that no such effect is known to exist in this kind of test, and questioned whether the idea had come from him at all.

**4:42** · NARRATOR: Don Vidrine insisted on repeating the test on the kill line, a narrow pipe from the rig down to the blowout preventer. Nothing flowed from it for thirty minutes. The drill pipe still read fourteen hundred.

**4:55** · NARRATOR: Around eight p.m., the test was declared a success.

**4:59** · NARRATOR: The commission later wrote that the pressure data were not ambiguous. In its view, the men had started from the assumption that the well could not be flowing.

**5:09** · NARRATOR: Four months earlier, a Transocean rig in the North Sea had a near miss after a negative test was declared a success. According to Transocean, the lessons never reached the Deepwater Horizon crew.

### 4 · The signs (5:21)

**5:21** · NARRATOR: At eight-oh-two, the crew began pumping the mud out and seawater in. Halliburton cementer Chris Haire was told the test was good, and to get ready for the final cement plug.

**5:33** · NARRATOR: As the heavy mud left the well, so did the weight holding back the reservoir. The cement at the bottom, investigators later concluded, was not holding. According to later computer models, at about eight-fifty, oil and gas began to enter the well.

**5:48** · NARRATOR: At nine-oh-one, the drill pipe pressure began to rise while the pumps ran steadily. Minutes later, with the pumps off, fluid kept flowing out. At about nine-ten, the returns were sent overboard, past the meters that measured them.

**6:04** · NARRATOR: The crew was busy with other work at the same time. Investigators concluded that the signs were missed.

**6:10** · NARRATOR: Around nine-twenty, senior toolpusher Randy Ezell phoned Jason Anderson on the rig floor. By Ezell's account, Anderson told him the test had gone fine: they had watched it for thirty minutes, with no flow.

**6:24** · NARRATOR: At about nine-thirty, driller Dewey Revette noticed a pressure difference, and the pumps were shut down. Ten minutes later, mud shot out of the riser onto the rig floor.

### 5 · The blowout (6:34)

**6:35** · NARRATOR: Gas was already above the blowout preventer, rising and expanding up the riser. The crew sent the flow to a mud-gas separator on the rig, rather than overboard, and closed the preventer. The separator was not built for a blowout. Gas spread over the rig.

**6:52** · NARRATOR: Crane operator Micah Sandell saw mud shooting all the way up the derrick, and then an explosion.

**6:59** · NARRATOR: On the supply vessel Damon B. Bankston, tied up alongside, Captain Alwin Landry saw mud falling on his deck, in his words, kind of like a black rain.

**7:10** · NARRATOR: Mike Williams was in his shop, below the rig floor.

**7:14** · MIKE WILLIAMS (testimony, DVIDS 91190, part 1, source 6:54–6:59): “What happened next was I heard a hissing noise and a thump.”

**7:19** · NARRATOR: He heard alarms beeping from the engine control room next door. Gas had reached the engines.

**7:26** · MIKE WILLIAMS (testimony, DVIDS 91190, part 1, source 8:58–9:14, 9:15–9:24, 9:30–9:52): “I could hear engine number three start to rev up, and it's normal operating RPMs to way above what I'd ever heard it run before. And it's continuously, steadily rising. And I knew then that we were having a problem. … As I start to push back from my desk, the computer monitor exploded in front of me. All the lights in my shop popped. The light bulbs themselves physically popped. … I reach down to grab my door, and at the simultaneously of grabbing the handle, the engine goes to a level that is higher than I can even describe it. It's spinning so fast that it just stopped spinning, and there was a huge explosion.”

**8:15** · NARRATOR: It was about nine forty-nine.

**8:18** · MIKE WILLIAMS (testimony, DVIDS 91191, part 2, source 0:03–0:12): “It blew the fire door and myself across the shop. And within seconds, the CO2 system started discharging.”

**8:27** · MIKE WILLIAMS (testimony, DVIDS 91191, part 2, source 1:55–2:09): “As I'm crawling through the ECR, I crawl across the bodies of, of at least two men. I don't know their condition. I'm trying to get them to respond. They're not responding. I assume they're dead and keep moving, because I know that I'm in no condition to help them.”

**8:41** · NARRATOR: He made it outside.

**8:43** · MIKE WILLIAMS (testimony, DVIDS 91191, part 2, source 5:06–5:20): “the, the hissing noise has now turned into a full-blown roar, and I look at the doghouse and the derrick. The doghouse is on fire and about half of the derrick at this point is on fire. It was then I realized we had a blowout.”

**8:58** · MIKE WILLIAMS (testimony, DVIDS 91191, part 2, source 5:36–5:44): “Once I got on the bridge, I reported immediately to the captain that we have no propulsion, we have no power, we have no ECR.”

### 6 · Abandon ship (9:07)

**9:08** · NARRATOR: Williams told investigators he never heard the general alarm.

**9:12** · MIKE WILLIAMS (testimony, DVIDS 91195, part 3, source 8:53–9:02): “Did you at any time hear any alarm, uh, on the, that would indicate a general muster or- Never.”

**9:21** · NARRATOR: Investigators found that the general alarm came minutes after the first gas alarms. Dynamic positioning officer Andrea Fleytas sent a Mayday.

**9:31** · NARRATOR: Two lifeboats left from the front of the rig. Others were cut off, and jumped fifty to seventy feet into the water. Williams stayed with a group trying to launch a life raft.

**9:42** · MIKE WILLIAMS (testimony, DVIDS 91195, part 3, source 2:41–2:51): “At that point, I wasn't sure that the life raft was gonna survive. There was so much heat coming up, w- I thought for sure the life raft was gonna pop or melt, and the people inside were gonna cook.”

**9:52** · MIKE WILLIAMS (testimony, DVIDS 91195, part 3, source 3:07–3:11, 3:32–3:38): “So there we set, three of us with no life raft and no lifeboats. … it was at that point that I informed the other two individuals that we can stay here and die or we can jump.”

**10:03** · MIKE WILLIAMS (testimony, DVIDS 91195, part 3, source 4:05–4:10, 4:13–4:15, 4:21–4:31): “I remember telling a, a, a young woman that was beside me that we're gonna have to run and jump. … I remember her responding saying that she couldn't jump, she couldn't jump, … I said, "He just did it. You've got to do it." She kept saying she couldn't do it, and I said, "Well, watch me then." And I took off running, and I jumped.”

**10:21** · MIKE WILLIAMS (testimony, DVIDS 91195, part 3, source 5:08–5:20): “At that point, I started backstroking with the one arm and one leg that would work until I remember feeling no pain. I remember feeling no heat and thinking that, uh, that was it, I had died.”

**10:34** · MIKE WILLIAMS (testimony, DVIDS 91195, part 3, source 5:43–5:46, 5:59–6:15): “And then I heard something in the distance. I heard, "Over here, over here." … At one point, I, I didn't hear the noise anymore, I didn't feel the pain anymore, and then I felt something start lifting me out of the water Uh, a small orange rescue craft, small boat had, had grabbed me and flipped me over into the boat.”

**10:54** · NARRATOR: The rescue boat had come from the Bankston. Its crew had backed the vessel away from the rig and launched the boat to pull people from the water.

**11:02** · MIKE WILLIAMS (testimony, DVIDS 91195, part 3, source 6:31–6:44): “He said, "I can't leave. There's more people in the water. I see more lights." So we went to the next light that we saw in the water and retrieved that person, who was, uh, Andrea, a dynamic position operator.”

**11:16** · NARRATOR: Andrea Fleytas, the officer who had sent the Mayday.

**11:20** · NARRATOR: All a hundred and fifteen survivors ended up on the Bankston. The first Coast Guard helicopter arrived a little after eleven p.m. Through the night, the most badly injured were lifted off and flown to shore.

**11:33** · NARRATOR: At the muster, eleven men were missing. The Coast Guard searched for three days. They were never found.

### 7 · The last line of defense (11:43)

**11:43** · NARRATOR: In the minutes after the explosions, subsea supervisor Chris Pleasant pressed the emergency disconnect on the bridge. It should have closed the blowout preventer's blind shear ram, the blades meant to cut the drill pipe and seal the well, and freed the rig from the riser. The lights on the panel changed. The rig did not disconnect.

**12:03** · NARRATOR: The preventer also had an automatic backup, called the deadman, meant to close the blades on its own if power, communication, and hydraulic pressure from the rig were lost. After the explosions, they were.

**12:17** · NARRATOR: Investigators did not fully agree on what happened next. The Chemical Safety Board concluded that the deadman most likely did fire that night, but that the drill pipe inside had buckled and been pushed off center. The blades caught the pipe at its edge, and could not seal the well.

**12:34** · NARRATOR: The forensic examination for the federal joint investigation also found the pipe off center, but concluded that the blades most likely closed two days later, when an underwater robot triggered them.

**12:46** · NARRATOR: The control pods that run the deadman also had wiring faults. The commission, writing before the forensic tests, thought these faults had kept it from working. The Chemical Safety Board concluded that it fired anyway. And by the time the crew closed the preventer, the commission noted, the flow may already have been too strong to stop. Earlier detection would have improved the odds.

**13:09** · NARRATOR: The blowout preventer was raised from the seafloor in September twenty ten, and taken as evidence.

### 8 · After (13:15)

**13:16** · NARRATOR: The Deepwater Horizon burned for about thirty-six hours. On the morning of April twenty-second, it sank.

**13:24** · NARRATOR: Oil flowed from the well for eighty-seven days. The government estimated that four point nine million barrels were released. A federal court later put the amount at about four million barrels.

**13:36** · NARRATOR: A capping stack stopped the flow on July fifteenth. On September nineteenth, after a relief well, the well was declared permanently sealed.

**13:45** · NARRATOR: BP pleaded guilty to fourteen criminal counts, including eleven counts of seaman's manslaughter, and agreed to pay four billion dollars in that case. A federal court later found it grossly negligent. Transocean pleaded guilty to a Clean Water Act violation.

**14:03** · NARRATOR: The federal agency that had overseen offshore drilling was broken up. New rules for blowout preventers and well control followed in twenty sixteen.

### 9 · The answer (14:12)

**14:13** · NARRATOR: So why was the well judged safe? Because the test that should have caught the problem was read as a success, with no standard for reading it, and with an explanation that was later found to have no basis. In fact, investigators concluded, the cement at the bottom had failed.

**14:30** · NARRATOR: And why didn't the blowout preventer stop it? The signs were missed until gas was already above it. When its blades closed, the drill pipe was in the wrong place for them to seal the well.

**14:42** · NARRATOR: The commission traced the disaster to a series of mistakes by BP, Halliburton, and Transocean, and to failures of management. It also wrote that the exact part each mistake played may never be known.
