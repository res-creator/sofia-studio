Status: draft for the owner's review. No narration recorded (ElevenLabs on the free tier), no render.
Working question: **"Catastrophic flooding was forecast days in advance. Why did so many people still end up trapped?"**
The answer must hold the real limits: what a forecast can and cannot say (scale, not streets); what officials decided and why (stated reasons, not blame); what people could actually do (water rose overnight, roads closed, 911 overwhelmed); what the reservoirs could do (hold water upstream or release it downstream).

## 1. The warning (Aug 23–25) ≈ 2:00

**Narrator:**
"Harvey had fallen apart once already, over the Caribbean. On Wednesday, August twenty-third, it came back to life in the Gulf of Mexico — and grew fast.
By Friday morning, the National Hurricane Center's advisories carried a new word: catastrophic.
That afternoon, forecasters put the Houston area under the highest flood risk they issue, three days ahead — a step their own review later called unprecedented.
What no forecast could say was which neighbourhoods, which streets, would take the worst of it."

**Then the decision:**
"Houston is a city of more than two million people, in a county of more than four and a half million. Coastal counties were ordered to leave. Houston was not.
The mayor, Sylvester Turner, asked residents to think twice before trying to leave Houston all at once. Harris County's top official, Ed Emmett, explained why: when a hurricane's storm surge comes in from the sea, you know who lives in its path. With rain, he said, you don't know which neighbourhoods are going to be affected.
They also remembered Hurricane Rita, twelve years earlier. Millions had tried to leave at once. Dozens of people died during that evacuation — twenty-three of them in a single bus fire on the highway — before Rita came ashore."

**Kiley's crew:** **Greene** (620838 0:01.9–0:12.6): "Things spun up super rapidly… flying our normal flight schedule Friday afternoon to canceling it… 'if we can get a crew on the road now…'"

## 3. The night the water rose (Aug 26–27) ≈ 2:00

**Narrator:**
"On Saturday night the rain bands settled over Houston. At 9:27 p.m., the weather service issued the first flash flood emergency. In parts of the city, about twenty-five inches fell in two days — most of it in under half a day.
The bayous rose out of their banks in the dark. Water came into houses while people slept. Roads that had been dry in the evening were under water by midnight.
By Sunday morning, 911 lines were overwhelmed — fifty-six thousand calls in fifteen hours. The county judge made an appeal: "If you have a boat, we need your help.""

## 5. The reservoirs (Aug 25–30) ≈ 2:30 — map sequence

Purpose: explain, step by step, what the dams did — **without** suggesting that opening the gates caused Houston's flood. About 154,000 houses flooded across Harris County, overwhelmingly from rain on its many bayous (HCFCD); the releases affected the Buffalo Bayou corridor west of downtown (about 4,000 homes, Governor's Commission).

Own map (built from USACE/USGS data — **not** the Google-Earth-based USACE animation 548079): west Houston, Addicks and Barker dams, Buffalo Bayou running east to downtown.

## 8. The answer ≈ 1:00

**Narrator:**
"So why were so many people trapped, after a warning that came in time?
Because the forecast was right about the scale of the disaster, but could not say which streets would flood — and the rain fell in hours, in the dark, faster than most people could react.
Because the safest choice for a city of millions was not obvious — and leaving had killed people before.
And because some of the water had nowhere left to go: holding it flooded one neighbourhood, releasing it flooded another.
What filled the gap was people — in helicopters, in trucks and in their own boats."

**Kiley** (1:42.6–2:02.6, over a wide hoist shot — labelled): "…you can see on the horizon, you just see helicopters and guys getting hoisted down, like, everywhere. It was overpowering sometimes, just seeing the effort."
Picture: 546164 0:45–1:15 (hoist operator at the door over flooded trees) → 546526 0:57–1:00 (people walking away from the landing zone) → end card.
