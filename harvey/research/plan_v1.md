### Flooding above the dams and the effect of the releases downstream — how the film explains it
1. **What the reservoirs are:** Addicks and Barker are dry federal flood-control reservoirs built in the 1940s west of Houston. Most of the time the land behind the dams is dry. The government owns only part of the area that can fill with water; since the 1940s, the design allowed water to rise onto private land beyond it. Over the decades, neighbourhoods were built on that private land inside the reservoirs.
2. **Holding the water (Aug 25–27):** the Corps closed the gates to protect Buffalo Bayou, downtown and the Ship Channel. The pools rose onto private land; homes **inside the reservoirs** flooded from the held water (Governor's Commission: more than 9,000 homes and businesses inside the reservoirs — no single official count).
3. **Releasing the water (from about midnight Aug 28):** with the pools rising toward record levels and water about to flow around the ends of the dams, the Corps opened the gates under its pre-written release rule. The releases added thousands of cubic feet per second to an already flooded Buffalo Bayou: homes **downstream** flooded or stayed flooded longer (about 4,000, Governor's Commission). Peak release: 13,800 cfs.
4. **Why — the stated reason:** dam safety. The Corps' worst-case estimate for a failure was 600,000 lives at risk; the court later found that, at the levels actually reached, the risk of failure was very low ("zero" below 111.6 ft in a Corps document). **Both are shown together.**
5. **Communication:** the state commission found that "inadequate communication regarding the impact of the dam releases compromised homeowner preparation and delayed evacuation until the area was already flooded." When and how residents were first told: not established (open question).
6. **What courts decided later — not blame for the decision:** upstream owners won a takings case (2019; affirmed on appeal Dec 22, 2025); downstream owners lost in 2020, won on appeal in 2022 and won liability at trial on April 22, 2026. The courts did not find that the Corps acted unreasonably; they found that particular owners had been made to bear a public burden and must be compensated. Whether the government sought Supreme Court review is unknown (open).

**How we avoid "they were warned but nobody listened":** the film shows the forecast's words as they were issued, the officials' stated reasons in their own words, and what the forecasters themselves later found (NWS assessment: forecasts gave "extensive lead time"; residents did not grasp what tens of inches of rain would mean; the forecast's heaviest-rain centre was about 110 miles south-west of where the heaviest rain fell). The question is what you can do with a warning that is right about the scale but cannot say which street floods.
