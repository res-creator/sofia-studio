PINNED COMMENT
Sources: Kearns, Schmidt & Glantz, "Sugar Industry and Coronary Heart Disease Research: A Historical Analysis of Internal Industry Documents", JAMA Internal Medicine (Sept 2016) — the Sugar Research Foundation approved "Project 226" on July 13, 1965 (a review of carbohydrates, fats and heart disease) by Harvard's Hegsted and McGandy, supervised by Fredrick Stare; the SRF set the review's objective and sent articles it wanted covered; the review ran in the New England Journal of Medicine in 1967 without disclosing the SRF funding; the SRF paid $6,500 (about $48,000 in 2016 dollars); Hegsted wrote in 1965: "We are well aware of your particular interest in carbohydrate…". STAT News (Sept 12, 2016) also has the Sugar Association's reply: the industry "should have exercised greater transparency", but it says sugar "does not have a unique role in heart disease". The 2016 authors note their evidence is indirect: the documents show who paid and what they asked for, not that the money changed the review's conclusions. All people in the video are generic drawings (re-enactment), not portraits.
Tip: when you read a study, look for the "funding" or "conflicts of interest" line.

YOUTUBE SHORTS
Title: The Sugar Industry Paid Harvard Scientists — And Nobody Was Told (1965–67)
Description: In 1965 the Sugar Research Foundation quietly paid Harvard scientists to review what really causes heart disease. Early studies were starting to point at sugar, so the industry set the question and sent the studies it wanted challenged. One scientist wrote back: "We are well aware of your particular interest." The review ran in the New England Journal of Medicine in 1967, played sugar down and named fat the main target — and readers were not told who paid. In 2016, researchers at UCSF found the letters in old archives. The price: $6,500 (about $48,000 in 2016 dollars). No one proved the money changed a word — but the science had a sponsor, and nobody was told. (Illustrated re-enactment; generic characters.)
