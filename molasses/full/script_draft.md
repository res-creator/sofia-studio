## COLD OPEN (approved text, ~1 min)

January fifteenth, 1919. Boston's North End.
It's just after noon, and the city is enjoying something rare... a warm day, in the middle of winter.
Above Commercial Street stands a steel tank. Fifty feet tall. Ninety feet wide. Inside it: more than two million gallons of molasses.
And the tank has been leaking for years. So badly, that the company painted it brown... to hide the streaks.
At around twelve thirty, the steel splits apart.
A wave of molasses, by some estimates twenty-five feet high, rolls through the streets at an estimated thirty-five miles an hour.
It flattens buildings. It bends the steel of the elevated railway. It catches people, horses, wagons... and it doesn't let go.
Twenty-one people will die. About a hundred and fifty will be injured.
And the company that owned the tank? It will blame anarchists.
This is the story of the Great Molasses Flood... and the warnings that nobody wanted to hear.

## CHAPTER 1 — A TANK BY THE HARBOR (~1 min 30 s)

To understand how this happened, we need to go back four years, to the edge of Boston Harbor.

In 1915, the Purity Distilling Company built a storage tank at 529 Commercial Street, in the North End. **[CHECK]**
The location made sense. Ships could tie up right next to it, unload their molasses, and pump it straight into the tank.
From there, the molasses was sent by pipe to the company's plant in Cambridge, across the river. **[CHECK]**

Why so much molasses? Because molasses can be fermented into ethanol.
Ethanol goes into alcoholic drinks. But it is also a key ingredient in munitions.
And in the years of the First World War, munitions were very good business.

In 1917, Purity was bought by a much bigger company: United States Industrial Alcohol, or USIA.
The tank on Commercial Street was now part of a national operation.
And the man responsible for it was not an engineer.

Picture the street. On one side, the harbor and the piers. Overhead, the steel frame of the elevated railway on Atlantic Avenue, with trains rattling past.
Around it, warehouses, a firehouse, homes, horses and wagons, and people going about their day.
And in the middle of it all, a fifty-foot wall of steel. **[CHECK — scene description; elements from the photos and the article]**

## CHAPTER 2 — BUILT IN A HURRY (~2 min)

His name was Arthur Jell. He was the company's treasurer. **[CHECK — some sources say assistant treasurer]**
Jell oversaw the construction of the tank. He had no background in architecture or engineering.
He was a financial man, placed in charge of a construction project that would hold millions of gallons of liquid. **[CHECK]**
Investigators would later describe the tank as poorly built and not tested enough. **[CHECK]**

Before a tank like this is filled with something heavy, the normal step is to test it.
You fill it with water, and you watch for leaks.
That test was never properly done. **[CHECK]**

The tank may have started leaking the very first time it was filled, in 1915.
And it kept leaking.
Molasses seeped through the seams and ran down the sides in long, dark streaks.
People in the neighborhood noticed. Some of them brought containers and collected the leaking molasses to take home. **[CHECK]**

The company's answer was not to fix the tank. It was to paint it brown.
Brown, like molasses. So the streaks would be harder to see.

There were other warnings, too. The tank made noises. Groaning, rumbling sounds, as the steel took the load.
Those warnings were ignored. **[CHECK]**
Jell heard about the noises. The tank stayed in service. **[CHECK — Wikipedia: Jell 'ignored warning noises']**

And there was pressure to keep the tank full.
Prohibition was coming. The amendment that would ban alcohol across the country was ratified on January sixteenth, 1919, one day after the disaster. **[CHECK]**
Several historians argue that the company was rushing to produce as much as it could before the law took effect. **[CHECK]**
The ban itself would begin one year later. But the clock was already running. **[CHECK]**

In its whole life, the tank was filled to capacity only eight times. **[CHECK]**
And on January fifteenth, 1919, it was holding more than two million gallons.

## CHAPTER 3 — THE WARM DAY (~1 min)

That winter had been bitterly cold.
In the days before the disaster, the temperature had been around two degrees Fahrenheit. **[CHECK]**
Then the weather turned. By January fifteenth, it was above forty degrees.
After weeks of freezing weather, it felt almost like spring.

The day before, a ship had delivered a fresh load of molasses.
That molasses had been warmed, so it would flow more easily. **[CHECK]**
Warm molasses, poured into a tank full of colder molasses, on a day when the air was suddenly mild.
Some researchers believe the expansion inside the tank added to the strain on the steel. **[CHECK]**
Liquids take up more space when they warm. Not much more. But in a tank holding more than two million gallons, a little is a lot.
And this was a tank that was already leaking, already groaning, built from steel that, as we'll see, was never strong enough.

## CHAPTER 4 — TWELVE THIRTY (~2 min)

At about twelve thirty, people on Commercial Street heard a sound they would never forget.
Some said it was a deep roar, like an elevated train passing overhead.
Others heard a crash. A growl. A bang like thunder.
And a rapid popping, like a machine gun. That was the rivets, shooting out of the steel. **[CHECK]**

The tank split open, and more than two million gallons of molasses came out at once.

The wave reached about twenty-five feet at its peak. Some accounts say fifteen feet. The memorial plaque says forty.
It moved at around thirty-five miles an hour.

Pieces of the tank's steel were thrown into the structure of the elevated railway on Atlantic Avenue. **[CHECK]**
A streetcar was briefly knocked off its tracks. **[CHECK]**
Buildings were pushed off their foundations. A truck was hurled into the harbor. **[CHECK]**
Several blocks were flooded two to three feet deep. **[CHECK]**

A boy named Anthony di Stasio was walking home from school.
The wave picked him up and carried him on its crest, then rolled him under as it slowed.
Anthony survived. **[CHECK]**

And this is what made the molasses so deadly.
As it spread and cooled in the winter air, it thickened.
People who were caught in it were trapped, unable to pull themselves free.

Think about what molasses is like on a cold morning.
Thick. Slow. Almost solid.
On that mild afternoon, the molasses came out of the tank warm and fast.
But the air was still January air. And as the wave spread out and lost its speed, it cooled.
The cooler it got, the thicker it became.
Within minutes, a fast-moving wave turned into something closer to wet cement. **[CHECK — description of thickening; 'wet cement' is a comparison, not a quote]**


## CHAPTER 5 — THE RESCUE (~2 min)

The first help came from the water.
A training ship of the Massachusetts Nautical School, the USS Nantucket, was docked nearby. **[CHECK]**
One hundred and sixteen cadets, led by Lieutenant Commander H. J. Copeland, ran to the scene. **[CHECK]**
They waded straight into the molasses and began pulling people out.

Boston police arrived. So did the Red Cross, and men from the Army and the Navy.
Red Cross nurses waded into the molasses as well.
A makeshift hospital was set up nearby. **[CHECK]**
One of the most common problems doctors saw was coughing: people had breathed in the molasses. **[CHECK]**

The search went on for four days. **[CHECK]**
Many of the dead were so covered in molasses that they were hard to identify.
Some victims had been swept into the harbor. They were not found for three to four months. **[CHECK]**

Twenty-one people died.
They were workers, drivers, firefighters, neighbors.
The youngest were two ten-year-olds: Maria Di Stasio and Pasquale Iantosca. **[CHECK]**
Among them was a firefighter, George Layhe. **[CHECK]**
Several horses died too. And about a hundred and fifty people were injured.

## CHAPTER 6 — A STICKY CITY (~1 min)

Then came the cleanup.
Workers sprayed the streets with salt water from a fireboat, and spread sand to soak up the molasses. **[CHECK]**
Several hundred people worked for weeks to clear the area. **[CHECK]**

The molasses didn't stay in the North End.
It was carried across the city on shoes and clothes.
It turned up on subway platforms, on streetcar seats, on telephones, in homes.
As one account put it: "Everything that a Bostonian touched was sticky." **[CHECK]**
The water of Boston Harbor stayed brown until the summer. **[CHECK]**

## CHAPTER 7 — "ANARCHISTS" (~2 min)

Now the question was: who was responsible?

USIA had an answer ready. The tank, it said, had been blown up. By anarchists.
It was not a random claim. Some of the alcohol made from that molasses was meant for munitions,
and in 1919, fear of political bombings was real. **[CHECK — context, not in the main source]**
If the tank had been attacked, the company would not be to blame.

The families did not accept that.
One hundred and nineteen residents took USIA to court, together. **[CHECK]**
It became one of the first class-action lawsuits in Massachusetts. **[CHECK]**
Today it's seen as a milestone in the history of corporate regulation. **[CHECK]**
For the first time, a company like USIA had to answer, in detail, for how it built and ran its equipment.

The court appointed an auditor to hear the case: Hugh W. Ogden. **[CHECK]**
The hearings lasted three years. **[CHECK]**
The anarchist theory was tested against the evidence: the leaks, the warnings, the steel.

In the end, Ogden found that USIA was responsible. **[CHECK]**
The company paid six hundred and twenty-eight thousand dollars in damages. **[CHECK]**
The families of those who died reportedly received about seven thousand dollars each. **[CHECK]**
Not a criminal conviction. Not a prison sentence. A bill.
But it was an answer to the question the company had tried to avoid: this was not an attack. It was a tank that should never have been trusted. **[CHECK — interpretation of the auditor's finding]**

## CHAPTER 8 — WHAT THE STEEL SAID (~1 min 30 s)

Almost a century later, researchers looked at the tank again.
A 2014 investigation found that the steel was only about half as thick as it should have been, even by the standards of the time. **[CHECK]**
The steel also lacked manganese, which made it more brittle. **[CHECK]**
And the cracks started at badly made rivet holes. **[CHECK]**

In 2016, a team at Harvard studied the flood using 1919 newspapers, maps and weather reports. **[CHECK]**
They even poured cold corn syrup over a scale model of the neighborhood.
Their conclusion: the speeds reported by witnesses were believable.
And the cold made the molasses thicken quickly, which made rescue so hard. **[CHECK]**

The disaster also changed the rules.
After the flood, construction laws changed.
Projects like this now needed to be overseen by a licensed architect and a civil engineer. **[CHECK]**
It sounds obvious today. A structure that holds millions of gallons should be designed and checked by people trained to do it.
In 1915, on Commercial Street, nobody had required that.

## CHAPTER 9 — THE PARK ON COMMERCIAL STREET (~1 min)

USIA never rebuilt the tank. **[CHECK]**
Today, the site is Langone Park. There's a Little League baseball field, a playground, and bocce courts. **[CHECK]**
A small plaque nearby remembers the disaster. **[CHECK]**

On the hundredth anniversary, in 2019, researchers used ground-penetrating radar under the ballfield.
About twenty inches down, they found the tank's concrete base. **[CHECK]**
At a ceremony, the names of all twenty-one victims were read aloud. **[CHECK]**

For decades, people in the North End said that on hot summer days, you could still smell molasses. **[CHECK]**

The tank leaked for years. It groaned. People saw it, and heard it.
And the answer was a coat of brown paint.
The Great Molasses Flood was not a freak of nature.
It was a warning... that nobody wanted to hear.

Twenty-one people. A hundred and fifty injured. A neighborhood buried in molasses.
Not because nobody knew. Because the people who knew decided it could wait.
So the next time you hear the Great Molasses Flood told as a funny story...
remember the streaks on the side of that tank. And the brown paint that covered them.
