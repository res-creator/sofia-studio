USIA never rebuilt the tank. Today, the site is Langone Park. There's a Little League baseball field, a playground, and bocce courts. A small plaque nearby remembers the disaster. On the hundredth anniversary in 2019, researchers used ground-penetrating radar under the ball field. About 20 inches down, they found the tank's concrete base. At a ceremony, the names of all 21 victims were read aloud. For decades, people in the North End said that on hot summer days, you could still smell molasses. The tank leaked for years. It groaned. People saw it and heard it, and the answer was a coat of brown paint. The Great Molasses Flood was not a freak of nature. It was a warning that nobody wanted to hear. 21 people, 150 injured, a neighborhood buried in molasses, not because nobody knew, because the people who knew decided it could wait. So the next time you hear the Great Molasses Flood told as a funny story, remember the streaks on the side of that tank and the brown paint that covered them.
