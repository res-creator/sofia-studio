To understand how this happened, we need to go back four years to the edge of Boston Harbor. In 1915, the Purity Distilling Company built a storage tank at 529 Commercial Street in the North End. The location made sense. Ships could tie up right next to it, unload their molasses, and pump it straight into the tank. From there, the molasses was sent by pipe to the company's plant in Cambridge across the river. Why so much molasses? Because molasses can be fermented into ethanol. Ethanol goes into alcoholic drinks, but it is also a key ingredient in munitions. And in the years of the First World War, munitions were very good business. In 1917, Purity was bought by a much bigger company, United States Industrial Alcohol, or USIA. The tank on Commercial Street was now part of a national operation, and the man responsible for it was not an engineer. Picture the street. On one side, the harbor and the piers. Overhead, the steel frame of the elevated railway on Atlantic Avenue with trains rattling past. Around it, warehouses, a firehouse, homes, horses and wagons, and people going about their day. And in the middle of it all, a fifty-foot wall of steel.
