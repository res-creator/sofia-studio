At about 12:30, people on Commercial Street heard a sound they would never forget. Some said it was a deep roar, like an elevated train passing overhead. Others heard a crash, a growl, a bang like thunder, and a rapid popping like a machine gun. That was the rivets shooting out of the steel. The tank split open, and more than two million gallons of molasses came out at once. The wave reached about twenty-five feet at its peak. Some accounts say fifteen feet. The memorial plaque says forty. It moved at around thirty-five miles an hour. Pieces of the tank's steel were thrown into the structure of the elevated railway on Atlantic Avenue. A streetcar was briefly knocked off its tracks. Buildings were pushed off their foundations. A truck was hurled into the harbor. Several blocks were flooded two to three feet deep. A boy named Anthony Destasio was walking home from school. The wave picked him up and carried him on its crest, then rolled him under as it slowed. Anthony survived, and this is what made the molasses so deadly. As it spread and cooled in the winter air, it thickened. People who were caught in it were trapped, unable to pull themselves free. Think about what molasses is like on a cold morning: thick, slow, almost solid. On that mild afternoon, the molasses came out of the tank warm and fast, but the air was still January air, and as the wave spread out and lost its speed, it cooled. The cooler it got, the thicker it became. Within minutes, a fast-moving wave turned into something closer to wet cement.
