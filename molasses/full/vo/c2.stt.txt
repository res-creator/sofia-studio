His name was Arthur Jell. He was the company's treasurer. Jell oversaw the construction of the tank. He had no background in architecture or engineering. He was a financial man, placed in charge of a construction project that would hold millions of gallons of liquid. Investigators would later describe the tank as poorly built and not tested enough. Before a tank like this is filled with something heavy, the normal step is to test it. You fill it with water, and you watch for leaks. That test was never properly done. The tank may have started leaking the very first time it was filled, in 1915, and it kept leaking. Molasses seeped through the seams and ran down the sides in long, dark streaks. People in the neighborhood noticed. Some of them brought containers and collected the leaking molasses to take home. The company's answer was not to fix the tank, it was to paint it brown. Brown, like molasses, so the streaks would be harder to see. There were other warnings, too. The tank made noises, groaning, rumbling sounds as the steel took the load. Those warnings were ignored. Jell heard about the noises. The tank stayed in service, and there was pressure to keep the tank full. Prohibition was coming. The amendment that would ban alcohol across the country was ratified on January 16th, 1919, one day after the disaster. Several historians argue that the company was rushing to produce as much as it could before the law took effect. The ban itself would begin one year later, but the clock was already running. In its whole life, the tank was filled to capacity only eight times, and on January 15th, 1919, it was holding more than two million gallons.
