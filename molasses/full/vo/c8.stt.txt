Almost a century later, researchers looked at the tank again. A 2014 investigation found that the steel was only about half as thick as it should have been, even by the standards of the time. The steel also lacked manganese, which made it more brittle, and the cracks started at badly made rivet holes. In 2016, a team at Harvard studied the flood using 1919 newspapers, maps, and weather reports. They even poured cold corn syrup over a scale model of the neighborhood. Their conclusion? The speeds reported by witnesses were believable, and the cold made the molasses thicken quickly, which made rescue so hard. The disaster also changed the rules. After the flood, construction laws changed. Projects like this now needed to be overseen by a licensed architect and a civil engineer. It sounds obvious today. A structure that holds millions of gallons should be designed and checked by people trained to do it. In 1915, on Commercial Street, nobody had required that.
