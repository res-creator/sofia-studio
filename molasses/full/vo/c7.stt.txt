Now the question was, who was responsible? USIA had an answer ready. The tank, it said, had been blown up by anarchists. It was not a random claim. Some of the alcohol made from that molasses was meant for munitions, and in 1919, fear of political bombings was real. If the tank had been attacked, the company would not be to blame. The families did not accept that. One hundred and nineteen residents took USIA to court together. It became one of the first class action lawsuits in Massachusetts. Today, it's seen as a milestone in the history of corporate regulation. For the first time, a company like USIA had to answer, in detail, for how it built and ran its equipment. The court appointed an auditor to hear the case, Hugh W. Ogden. The hearings lasted three years. The anarchist theory was tested against the evidence, the leaks, the warnings, the steel. In the end, Ogden found that USIA was responsible. The company paid six hundred and twenty-eight thousand dollars in damages. The families of those who died reportedly received about seven thousand dollars each. Not a criminal conviction, not a prison sentence, a bill. But it was an answer to the question the company had tried to avoid. This was not an attack. It was a tank that should never have been trusted.
