## PART 1 — THE PRICE THAT MAKES NO SENSE (0:35–2:20) [light paper-map chapter]
p01 This is a real one-way fare search an airline blogger published in January 2015, on United, from Los Angeles.
p02 A ticket to Washington Dulles: five hundred eighty-eight dollars.
p03 A ticket to Boston... that flies the exact same flight to Dulles on the way: two hundred sixteen.
  [two boarding passes; $588 vs $216 · ≈ $800 vs ≈ $295 TODAY]
p04 Same plane to Dulles. Less than half the price. And a free extra flight you don't want.
p05 So people figured out the obvious move: buy the Boston ticket... and walk out of the airport in Washington.
  [figure walks off at the layover; the Boston leg flies on with one empty seat]
p06 It's called hidden-city ticketing. Or, after the website we'll meet in a minute, "skiplagging."
p07 So why would the longer trip be cheaper? Because airlines don't price by distance. Or by cost.
p08b If they did, Boston would cost more. That's how math normally works.
p08 They price by market: what people flying from A to B will pay.
p09 Washington is a hub. Lots of people need to fly there nonstop, and they'll pay a premium for it.
  [plane icons stacking into DC, price tag grows]
p11 Boston, through a connection? Now the airline competes with every other airline's nonstop to Boston. So it cuts the price to win you.
  [rival planes (generic, no logos) race to Boston; tag drops]
p12 The connecting passenger is a bargain hunter. The nonstop passenger is a cash machine. And both are sitting on the same plane.
  [plane cross-section: seats tagged $$$ and $]
p13 That mix is how hubs make money. Which means a cheap ticket to Boston only works for the airline if you actually go to Boston.
p14 Skip the last flight, and you get the cheap price... on the expensive trip.
p15 The airline's position: you must fly to Boston. Whether you want to be in Boston is not their problem.

  [highlight: COPYRIGHT → THE LOGO (generic plane-symbol placeholder, never the real logo)]
v08 And four point seven million, taken from Skiplagged's own revenue.

  [highlight: NOT THE TRICK. THE LOGO.]
