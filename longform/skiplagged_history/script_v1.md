## PART 1 — THE PRICE THAT MAKES NO SENSE (0:50–2:40) [light paper-map chapter]
p01 Here's a real search from 2015, from Los Angeles.
p02 A ticket to Washington Dulles: five hundred eighty-eight dollars.
p03 A ticket to Boston... that stops in Washington Dulles on the way: two hundred fifteen.
  [two boarding passes side by side; $588 vs $216; ≈ $780 vs $285 TODAY]
p04 Same plane to Dulles. Same seat, maybe. Less than half the price — and you get a free extra flight you don't want.
p05 So people figured out the obvious move: buy the Boston ticket... and walk out of the airport in Washington.
  [figure walks off at the layover, the Boston leg flies away empty-seated]
p06 It's called hidden-city ticketing. Or, after the website we'll meet in a minute, "skiplagging."
p07 Why would an airline price it like that? Because airlines don't price by distance. And not by cost, either.
p08 They price by market: what people flying from A to B will pay.
p09 Washington is a hub. Lots of people need to fly there nonstop — business travelers, government, people who can't take a detour.
p10 That's a market where the airline can charge a premium.
p11 Boston, through a connection? Now the airline competes with every other airline's nonstop to Boston. So it cuts the price to win you.
p12 The connecting passenger is a bargain hunter. The nonstop passenger is a cash machine. And both are sitting on the same plane.
  [one plane cross-section: half the seats tagged $$$, half tagged $]
p13 That mix is how hubs make money. Which means a cheap ticket to Boston only works for the airline if you actually go to Boston.
p14 Skip the last flight, and you get the cheap price... on the expensive trip.
p15 Joke beat: the airline's official position is that you must fly to Boston. Whether you want to be in Boston is not their problem.

## PART 2 — THE KID WITH THE SEARCH ENGINE (2:40–4:40) [evidence desk]
k01 For years, hidden-city fares were a trick for travel nerds: you had to hunt for them by hand.
k02 Then, in 2013, a 20-year-old in New York named Aktarer Zaman built a site that hunted for them automatically.
  [RECEIPT 02: site card, tag 2013 · founder drawn as a generic figure]
k03 He called it Skiplagged. Type in where you want to go, and it shows you the flights that pass through it.
k04 As he put it: the trick had been around for a while. It just wasn't very accessible.
k05 In November 2014, it got accessible enough. United Airlines and the travel site Orbitz sued him.
  [complaint highlight: UNFAIR COMPETITION · $75,000 · ≈ $105K TODAY]
k06 They wanted seventy-five thousand dollars in lost revenue and an order to stop him.
k07 United's argument: hidden-city tickets break its rules, and cause "logistical and public safety concerns."
k08 Zaman was 22. He asked the internet for help. "A 22-year-old with a startup being bullied by these large corporations," he wrote.
k09 The internet answered. In days, more than sixteen hundred strangers chipped in thirty-three thousand dollars for his lawyers.
  [counter climbing; donor ticks]
k10 And the lawsuit did something United definitely didn't plan: it made Skiplagged famous.
  [analysis tag: OUR TAKE]
k11 Millions of people who'd never heard of hidden-city tickets now knew exactly where to find them.
k12 In 2015, a federal judge threw United's case out. Not because Skiplagged was right — because United had sued in the wrong state.
  [stamp DISMISSED · small: WRONG VENUE (ILLINOIS)]
k13 Orbitz settled. United didn't try again.
k14 Round one: the kid.

## PART 3 — THE AIRLINES GO AFTER YOU (4:40–7:00) [desk + map inserts]
a01 If you can't stop the website... you can go after the people using it.
a02 In 2018, a United passenger got a letter from the airline's Corporate Security department.
  [RECEIPT 03: re-created letter, highlight "Point Beyond Ticketing"]
a03 Subject: "Notice of Claim... and Demand for Reimbursement."
a04 United said it had found thirty-eight trips since 2016 where he hadn't flown the whole ticket. It called that "fraud."
a05 The bill: three thousand, two hundred thirty-six dollars and seventy-six cents. Pay within ten business days...
  [$3,236.76 · ≈ $4,200 TODAY]
a06 ...or United might send it to a collection agency, shut down his frequent-flyer account, and refuse to fly him again.
a07 A debt, for flights he paid for and didn't take. That's a strange kind of debt.
a08 In Germany, Lufthansa went further. It took a passenger to court.
  [map: Oslo – Frankfurt – Seattle; on the way back the last leg Frankfurt → Oslo is crossed out, he flies to Berlin]
a09 He'd booked a round trip from Oslo to Seattle through Frankfurt — about seven hundred dollars instead of roughly three thousand for the trip he really wanted.
a10 On the way home, he got off in Frankfurt and flew to Berlin instead.
a11 Lufthansa sued him for two thousand one hundred twelve euros.
a12 A Berlin court ruled against the airline. Lufthansa appealed... then quietly dropped the appeal.
a13 The passenger's lawyer said Lufthansa had realized its own rules didn't hold up under German law.
  [stamp CASE CLOSED · tag OCT 2019]
a14 And then, in July 2023, the story nobody at American Airlines wanted on the news.
a15 A 17-year-old had a ticket from Gainesville, Florida, to New York — with a stop in Charlotte. Charlotte, where he lived.
  [map GNV → CLT → NYC; home pin at CLT]
a16 At check-in, an agent looked at his North Carolina driver's license... and did the math.
a17 The ticket was cancelled. His dad says the airline banned him for three years.
a18 American's rule: you can't buy a ticket "without intending to fly all flights to gain lower fares."
a19 The skiplag ticket had cost a hundred and fifty dollars. The direct flight the family bought instead? Four hundred.
  [two receipts: $150 vs $400]
a20 So the punishment for paying too little... was paying more. Lesson learned. By someone.

## PART 4 — THE NINE-POINT-FOUR-MILLION-DOLLAR VERDICT (7:00–8:50) [evidence desk, red-string board]
v01 Meanwhile, the airlines kept coming for the website.
v02 Southwest sued Skiplagged in 2021. That one settled.
v03 In 2023, American Airlines sued. And this time, the case went all the way to a jury in Fort Worth, Texas.
  [RECEIPT 04: AMERICAN AIRLINES v. SKIPLAGGED · tag FORT WORTH · 2023]
v04 American threw everything in: trademark, contracts, copyright.
v05 In October 2024, the jury decided. Nine point four million dollars, for American.
  [counter $9,400,000 · ≈ $9.8M TODAY]
v06 Here's the part most headlines skipped. Look at what the money was actually for.
  [split bar: 4.7 / 4.7]
v07 Four point seven million, for copyright — because Skiplagged copied American's flight schedules, against the rules of American's website.
v08 And four point seven million, taken from Skiplagged's own revenue.
v09 American's trademark claim — that Skiplagged was using its logo to look official? Rejected.
v10 So the jury didn't say hidden-city tickets are illegal. The verdict was about how the site got its data — and the money it made.
  [highlight: NOT THE TRICK. THE DATA.]
v11 In 2025, a judge made the nine-point-four million final. And the trick itself? Still not illegal.

## PART 5 — SO... SHOULD YOU DO IT? (8:50–10:10) [light paper section]
s01 Here's the honest version.
s02 It only works one way. Skip a flight, and the airline usually cancels the rest of your trip, including your return.
s03 No checked bags. Your suitcase is going to Boston, even if you're not.
  [suitcase rides the plane alone, tiny wave]
s04 If the flight gets rerouted through a different city, your plan goes with it.
s05 Do it often, and you can lose your miles, your account, or — like that teenager — your airline.
s06 And there's a bigger catch. If enough people skiplag, the airline can't keep that cheap seat cheap.
s07 Experts who defend the airlines say the answer won't be fairer prices. It'll be fewer nonstop flights, smaller planes, or higher fares to everyone else.
s08 So the airlines aren't wrong that it costs them money.
s09 But think about what they're actually defending: a price that says the shorter trip is worth more than double the longer one.
s10 You'd have bought that Boston ticket too.
