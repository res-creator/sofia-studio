## COLD OPEN — "The pile" (0:00–0:55)

**h1** In 2004, inside a toy company in a small town in Denmark, someone tried to count all the different LEGO pieces the company was making. [22,35]

**h2** Not the bricks. The *kinds* of bricks. The answer was more than twelve thousand. And most of the new ones had been used in exactly one set. [22,35]

**h3** That same year, the company that made the most famous toy on Earth lost almost two billion Danish kroner. About three hundred million dollars. [28]

**h4** Its sales had collapsed. Its biggest customers were complaining. And the man asked to fix it was a thirty-five-year-old former consultant who had never run a company. [24,30,33]

**h5** So how does a company with the most loved toy in the world nearly destroy itself? To understand that, you have to go back to a carpenter... and a fire.

## CHAPTER 1 · The Carpenter — Billund, 1932 (0:55–2:45)

**c1** In 1932, in the middle of the Great Depression, a carpenter in Billund named Ole Kirk Christiansen was running out of work. Farmers weren't buying furniture. So he started making something smaller: wooden toys. Ducks on wheels. Trucks. Piggy banks. [1]

**c2** Two years later, he gave the business a name made from two Danish words, "leg godt." Play well. LEGO. [2]

**c3** In 1942, his workshop burned to the ground. He rebuilt it. [3]

**c4** Then, after the war, he made his strangest bet yet. In 1947, LEGO bought a plastic injection-molding machine. It reportedly cost more than twice what the company had earned the year before. For a toy maker in a tiny Danish town, plastic was a gamble. [4]

**c5** By 1949, the machine was making little plastic bricks that stacked on top of each other. They were called Automatic Binding Bricks. They had one problem. They didn't stay together very well. [5]

**c6** Ole Kirk's son, Godtfred, solved it. He added tubes under the brick. Now the studs on top locked into the tubes below. On January 28th, 1958, at 1:58 in the afternoon, the company filed the patent. [6]

**c7** Ole Kirk died that same year. Two years later, another fire destroyed the warehouse of wooden toys. The company never went back. From then on, LEGO was plastic... and the brick was the whole idea. [7,8]

**c8** In 1955, LEGO had started selling the bricks as a whole "system of play." Every piece fits every other piece. A brick made today still clicks onto a brick made in 1958. Remember that. It's going to matter. [9]

## CHAPTER 2 · The Golden Years — 1978–1993 (2:45–4:05)

**g1** In 1978, the bricks got people. Little figures with yellow faces and claw-like hands that could hold a tool. Suddenly, every set was a story: a police station, a castle, a space base. [10]

**g2** For about fifteen years, LEGO grew fast. Around fourteen percent a year. Sales roughly doubled every five years. The company was now run by the founder's grandson, Kjeld Kirk Kristiansen. [11,12]

**g3** But the patents that protected the brick were expiring, country by country. Copies appeared. Same studs, same size, lower prices. And when LEGO went to court in Canada, the judges said the shape itself couldn't be owned forever. [13]

**g4** The world around the box was changing too. Kids were leaving toys earlier for video games. Giant chains like Walmart and Toys "R" Us decided what got shelf space. Rivals were making toys in China, while LEGO made bricks in Denmark, paid in a strong Danish currency. [15]

**g5** From 1993, LEGO kept launching more and more toys. Roughly three times as many new products. Sales didn't grow. Costs did. [14]

**g6** In 1998, for the first time in its history, LEGO lost money. About a thousand people were let go. [16]

## CHAPTER 3 · Beyond the Brick — 1998–2002 (4:05–6:05)

**b1** The family brought in a turnaround manager, Poul Plougmann. And a dangerous idea took hold: maybe kids didn't want bricks anymore. Maybe LEGO had to become something bigger than the brick. [18]

**b2** So LEGO went everywhere. Theme parks. Clothes. Video games. Television. And for the first time, other people's stories: Star Wars in 1999, Harry Potter a couple of years later. [19]

**b3** Some of it worked. Star Wars sets flew off the shelves. Bionicle, a line of buildable warrior figures with their own legend, became a hit. [19,21]

**b4** Other ideas forgot what LEGO was for. Explore, a line for toddlers, barely asked them to build anything. Jack Stone came with big chunky pieces that snapped together in minutes. And Galidor... was a buildable action figure with its own TV show, its own video game and a fast-food tie-in. It flopped. [20]

**b5** Meanwhile, inside the company, designers were free to invent. And they invented pieces. A new shape here. A new color there. In 1997, LEGO made about six thousand different elements. By 2004, it was more than fourteen thousand. [22]

**b6** Every new piece needs its own steel mold. Its own space in the warehouse. Its own line in the plan. And roughly nine out of ten new pieces ended up in just one set. [22]

**b7** The colors exploded too. At the peak, LEGO sets used more than a hundred different colors. [23]

**b7b** Picture a warehouse where every shelf holds a part that one set, one time, needed. That was the cost nobody saw on the box.

**b8** For a while, none of it showed. In 1999 and 2002 there were new Star Wars movies. In 2001 and 2002, Harry Potter. The blockbusters sold a lot of sets. They also hid the problem. [25]

## CHAPTER 4 · The Year Without a Movie — 2003 (6:05–7:35)

**y1** Then came 2003. No new Star Wars movie. No new Harry Potter movie. [25]

**y2** That year, LEGO's sales fell by more than a quarter. In the United States, by about a third. More than half of the drop came from the movie sets. Stores that had stocked up now cut their orders. [24,26]

**y3** And LEGO is a business with very high fixed costs. The factories, the molds, the warehouses, the people... all of it costs the same whether the sets sell or not. When sales drop, the losses arrive fast. [27]

**y4** The company announced a record loss: about one point four billion kroner before tax. The turnaround manager was out. Kjeld Kirk Kristiansen took the controls himself. [27]

**y5** When the finance team looked closely, the numbers were even worse. Sixty percent of the company's sales were producing all of its operating profit. The rest, put together, was losing money. One finance chief later put it bluntly: LEGO had been destroying value since 1993. [29]

**y6** And the 2004 numbers were on their way. Worse than 2003. For the first time in its history, it was a real question whether LEGO would survive. [28]

## CHAPTER 5 · The Outsider — 2004–2005 (7:35–9:25)

**o1** In October 2004, the family did something it had never done. It handed the company to someone outside the family. Jørgen Vig Knudstorp. Thirty-five. A former McKinsey consultant who had joined LEGO only three years earlier. [33]

**o2** His first job wasn't to make LEGO grow. It was to keep it alive. Inside the company, the rescue plan got a name: Shared Vision. And its first phase was blunt. Manage for cash. Stop the bleeding before anything else. [31,37]

**o2b** The family stayed on as owners, and Kjeld Kirk Kristiansen backed the outsider, even when the cuts reached things the family had built itself. [33]

**o3** The theme parks went first. In 2005, LEGO sold seventy percent of Legoland to the investment firm Blackstone, for three hundred seventy-five million euros. [34]

**o4** Then the pieces. Designers were told to build with what already existed. The catalog of elements was cut from roughly thirteen thousand to about seven thousand. [35]

**o5** Then the sets. Back to the basics: police stations, fire trucks, race cars. Things kids actually build. [36]

**o6** LEGO went back to the retailers who had called it unfocused and self-centered, and started building the year around what they needed. [30,37]

**o7** And he listened to an audience the company had long ignored: adults. In 1998, a student had hacked LEGO's new robot brick and published how it worked. LEGO didn't sue. In 2005, the new CEO went to a fan convention himself. [17,39]

**o8** It worked faster than almost anyone expected. In 2005, LEGO was back in profit: five hundred and five million kroner. [38]

## CHAPTER 6 · The Mistake Inside the Rescue — 2006–2009 (9:25–10:10)

**m1** But the rescue had one more twist. To cut costs, LEGO handed most of its brick production to an outside manufacturer, Flextronics, with factories in Mexico, Hungary and the Czech Republic. [40]

**m2** It didn't go smoothly. Molds and materials arrived before the factories were ready. Some parts ran short. [40]

**m3** In 2008, LEGO ended the deal and took the factories back. Its operations chief later summed up the lesson: you have to know what your company's core competence is. For LEGO, it turned out, making the brick was part of it. [40]

## CHAPTER 7 · The Brick — after 2008 (10:10–11:20)

**a1** What came next is hard to believe if you remember 2004. Over the following five years, sales grew about a quarter a year. [41]

**a2** In 2014, a movie made of LEGO bricks earned close to half a billion dollars. That year, for the first half of the year, LEGO passed Mattel and became the biggest toy company in the world. [42]

**a3** In 2019, the family's investment company led the deal to buy the Legoland parks back. [43]

**a4** And in 2025, LEGO reported record revenue: more than eighty-three billion kroner. [44]

**a5** So what nearly destroyed LEGO? Not one thing. Expiring patents. Video games. Big-box stores. A strong currency. A year without a blockbuster. And thousands of pieces that were made for one set... and never used again.

**a6** The rescue wasn't a new invention. It was the oldest thing in the building. A brick from 1958... that still fits.
