## COLD OPEN — One set, one piece (0:00–0:41)

**h1** By 2004, LEGO had invented thousands of new pieces. And most of them were used in exactly one set. [22]

**h2** That same year, the company behind the world's most famous toy lost almost two billion Danish kroner. About three hundred million dollars. [28]

**h3** Those two facts are connected. And the man who would have to untangle them was thirty-five years old, and had joined LEGO only three years earlier, from a consulting firm. [33]

**h4** To see how a box of bricks nearly sank its own maker, you have to start with one word that LEGO lived by. System.

## CHAPTER 1 · The System — 1932–1960 (0:41–2:11)

**c1** LEGO began in 1932 as a carpenter's workshop in Billund, Denmark, making wooden toys during the Depression. The name came from two Danish words: "leg godt." Play well. [1,2]

**c2** In 1947, the carpenter, Ole Kirk Christiansen, bought a plastic injection-molding machine. It reportedly cost more than twice what the company had earned the year before. By 1949, it was making little plastic bricks. [4,5]

**c3** According to LEGO's own history, in 1954 his son Godtfred heard a toy buyer complain that toys had no system. Nothing connected to anything else. So Godtfred built one. A town to build, where every piece fits every other piece, and every new set adds to the ones you already have. LEGO launched it the next year. [new F1, F2]

**c4** There was just one problem. The bricks didn't hold together very well. So Godtfred added tubes under each brick. In 1958, LEGO filed the patent. Now every brick locked. [6]

**c5** Two years later, a fire destroyed the company's warehouse of wooden toys. LEGO didn't rebuild that part. It focused on the bricks. [8]

**c6** Keep those two ideas in mind. A system where the same pieces keep coming back, set after set. And a company that grew stronger by doing less. Four decades later, LEGO would forget both. [plant]

## CHAPTER 2 · Too Successful — 1978–1998 (2:11–3:09)

**g1** The system worked for decades. In 1978, the bricks got little figures, and every set became a story. For about fifteen years, sales roughly doubled every five years. The company was now run by the founder's grandson, Kjeld Kirk Kristiansen. [10,11,12]

**g2** Then the ground started moving. The patents on the brick were expiring, and cheaper look-alikes appeared. Kids were turning to video games earlier. And the toy business was now decided by giant chains like Walmart and Toys "R" Us. [13,15]

**g3** LEGO's answer was more. From 1993, it roughly tripled the number of new toys it put out. Sales didn't grow. Costs did. [14]

**g4** In 1998, LEGO lost money for the first time in its history. About a thousand people lost their jobs. And the company started looking for someone to change it. [16]

## CHAPTER 3 · Beyond the Brick — 1998–2002 (3:09–5:31)

**b1** The family brought in a turnaround manager named Poul Plougmann. The diagnosis sounded reasonable: children were changing faster than LEGO was. Maybe a box of bricks wasn't enough anymore. [18]

**b2** So LEGO set out to become more than a brick company. Theme parks. Clothing. Video games. Television. And, for the first time, other people's worlds: Star Wars sets in 1999. Harry Potter in 2001. [19]

**b3** At first, it looked brilliant. The Star Wars sets sold enormously. A new line of buildable warriors called Bionicle became a hit. [19,21]

**b4** In 2001, LEGO also hired a young economist from McKinsey into its strategy department. His name was Jørgen Vig Knudstorp. For now, he was one person watching the plan from the inside. [33]

**b5** And inside, the plan was creating something nobody saw on the shelves. Designers were free to invent. And much of what they invented was new pieces. A special piece for this set. A new color for that one. [22]

**b6** Every new piece needs its own steel mold. Its own bin in the warehouse. Its own line in the factory schedule. In the late nineties, LEGO made about six thousand different elements. Within a few years, it was more than twelve thousand. And roughly nine out of ten new pieces were used just once. [22,35]

**b7** Every one of those new pieces still clicked onto the old bricks. That was never the problem. The problem was that most of them were needed just once, and each one still had to be molded, stored and tracked.

**b8** Some new toys hardly needed building at all. Explore, a line for toddlers. Jack Stone, with big chunky parts that snapped together in minutes. And Galidor, a buildable action figure with its own TV show, video game and fast-food tie-in. It flopped. [20]

**b9** Why didn't the numbers scream? Because of the movies. New Star Wars films came out in 1999 and 2002. Harry Potter in 2001 and 2002. Each one sold boxes of LEGO. And covered the cracks. [25]

## CHAPTER 4 · The Year Without a Movie — 2003–2004 (5:31–6:54)

**y1** In 2003, there was no new Star Wars movie. And no new Harry Potter movie. [25]

**y2** LEGO's sales fell by about a quarter. In the United States, by about a third. More than half of the drop came from the movie sets. And stores that had stocked up the year before now ordered less. [24,26]

**y3** That's when the hidden cost showed up. Every one of those thousands of molds, bins and production runs still had to be paid for, whether a set sold or not. [27]

**y4** In January 2004, LEGO announced a record loss for 2003: about one point four billion kroner before tax. The turnaround manager left. Kjeld Kirk Kristiansen took over the day-to-day running of the company himself. [27]

**y5** When the finance team went through the company product by product, they found something worse. About sixty percent of sales were producing all of the company's operating profit. Everything else, together, was losing money. One finance executive later said LEGO had been destroying value since 1993. [29]

**y6** And 2004 was turning out even worse. For the first time, it was a real question whether LEGO, as an independent family company, would survive. [28]

## CHAPTER 5 · The Outsider — 2004–2005 (6:54–8:42)

**o1** In October 2004, Kjeld Kirk Kristiansen stepped aside as chief executive. The family handed the company to someone outside the family for the first time. The young man from the strategy department. Jørgen Vig Knudstorp. He was thirty-five. [33]

**o2** He had spent three years studying how LEGO made and lost money. His first goal was not growth. It was survival. Get cash under control, and cut what the company couldn't afford. [31,37]

**o3** The family stayed on as owners, and backed him. Even when the cuts reached Legoland, the park the family had opened in Billund in 1968. In 2005, LEGO sold seventy percent of its four Legoland parks to the investment firm Blackstone, for three hundred seventy-five million euros. [34, new F3]

**o4** Then the pieces. Designers were told to work with what already existed. The catalog of different elements was cut roughly in half, to about seven thousand. [35]

**o5** With fewer pieces, the sets went back to what kids actually build: police stations, fire trucks, race cars. [36]

**o6** LEGO went back to the big retailers that had called it unfocused and self-centered, and planned its year around what they needed. [30]

**o7** And it started listening to fans it had mostly ignored: adults. Years earlier, in 1998, a student had cracked open LEGO's new robot brick and published how it worked. LEGO didn't sue. In 2005, the new chief executive went to a fan convention himself. [17,39]

**o8** It worked faster than almost anyone expected. In 2005, LEGO was back in profit: about five hundred million kroner. [38]

## CHAPTER 6 · The Fix That Broke — 2006–2009 (8:42–9:21)

**m1** But a rescue can make mistakes too. To cut costs further, LEGO moved a large share of its production to a contract manufacturer, Flextronics, in Hungary, the Czech Republic and Mexico. [40]

**m2** It did not go smoothly. Molds arrived before the factories were ready. Some parts ran short. [40]

**m3** In 2008, LEGO ended the deal and took production back. Its operations chief later put the lesson simply: a company has to know what its core competence is. For LEGO, making the brick was part of it. [40]

## CHAPTER 7 · It Still Fits — after 2008 (9:21–10:31)

**a1** Over the next five years, sales grew about a quarter a year. [41]

**a2** In 2014, a movie made of LEGO bricks earned close to half a billion dollars. In the first half of that year, LEGO passed Mattel and became the biggest toy company in the world. [42]

**a3** So what nearly destroyed LEGO? Not one bad decision. A changing market pushed it to do more. Doing more filled its factories with pieces that were used once. The movies hid the cost. And when the movies stopped, the cost was all that was left.

**a4** The way out wasn't a new idea. It was the oldest one in the company. Fewer pieces, used again and again.

**a5** And through all of it, the copies, the losses, the movies, the rescue, one thing never changed. A LEGO brick made today... still clicks onto one made in 1958. [9]

**a6** The bricks never stopped fitting together. For a few years, the company did.
