## COLD OPEN — Paris, March 1932 (0:00–0:50)

**h1** On a Saturday in March 1932, in his apartment in Paris, one of the most powerful businessmen in the world was found dead. The police ruled it suicide. [37,38]

**h2** His name was Ivar Kreuger. His companies made about two thirds of the world's matches. He had lent nearly four hundred million dollars to some fifteen governments. [13,19]

**h3** A few weeks later, accountants opened his books. Profits he had reported for years had never existed. About two hundred and fifty million dollars of his assets had never existed either. [40,41,42]

**h4** How did a man who sold matches fool banks, investors, and whole countries? It started with something every home in the world needed. And with a very good idea about money.

## CHAPTER 1 · The Engineer — 1880–1913 (0:50–2:00)

**k1** Ivar Kreuger was born in 1880 in Kalmar, a small town in Sweden. His family owned match factories. He studied engineering in Stockholm, and at twenty he left to see the world. [1,2,3]

**k2** For seven years he worked abroad, much of it in the United States. According to a 1933 profile in Fortune, as a young engineer in New York he worked on buildings like the Flatiron. [3,4,5]

**k3** Back home, in 1908, he and a partner, Paul Toll, started a building company. They worked fast, and they worked in a new material: reinforced concrete. Their firm helped build the stadium for the 1912 Stockholm Olympics. [7,8]

**k4** But Kreuger wasn't interested in buildings for long. He was interested in his family's old business. Matches.

## CHAPTER 2 · The Match Trust — 1913–1923 (2:00–3:15)

**t1** A match costs almost nothing. But everyone needs them, every single day: for stoves, lamps, cigarettes. Sell enough of them, everywhere, and a tiny profit becomes a fortune.

**t2** In 1913, Kreuger began buying up Swedish match factories. In 1917, he merged the biggest of them into one company: Swedish Match. He was its president. [10,11]

**t3** Then he went looking for money where there was the most of it. In 1923, with a respected Boston bank, Lee, Higginson, he set up a company in the United States: International Match. American investors could now buy a piece of the match empire. [20]

**t4** They bought. Over the next years, Americans put about two hundred and fifty million dollars into Kreuger's securities. They became some of the most widely held investments in the world. [21,22]

## CHAPTER 3 · Matches for Money — 1925–1930 (3:15–4:45)

**m1** After the First World War, many European governments were broke. Kreuger offered them a deal. I'll lend you dollars. In return, you give my company the right to be your country's only match seller. [15]

**m2** The first was Poland, in 1925: six million dollars. Then France, in 1927: seventy-five million. In 1929, Germany: one hundred and twenty-five million dollars, the biggest loan of all. In return, Germany kept cheap Russian matches out of the country. [15,16,17]

**m2b** And where did the money for those loans come from? Much of it from American investors. To lend to France, Kreuger's American company sold fifty million dollars of its own bonds in the United States. Money from Main Street went to governments in Europe, and came back as interest and monopolies. On paper, it was a perfect circle. [16,21]

**m3** Greece, Hungary, Yugoslavia, Romania, Latvia, Ecuador, Turkey… By 1930, he had lent nearly four hundred million dollars to some fifteen governments. [18,19]

**m4** And the matches followed. That year, by his own company's count, it controlled about sixty percent of the world's match production, in thirty-three countries. Other estimates were even higher. [12,13]

**m5** A man who lends money to entire countries looks like the safest borrower on Earth. That was the real product Kreuger sold. Not matches. Trust.

## CHAPTER 4 · The Match Palace — 1928–1929 (4:45–5:55)

**p1** In 1928, he opened his new headquarters in Stockholm. People called it the Match Palace. [27]

**p2** He had a penthouse on Park Avenue in New York, with trees on the roof. A twenty-room apartment above Place Vendôme in Paris. Homes in Berlin and Stockholm. A private island. [28]

**p3** In October 1929, his face was on the cover of Time magazine. It was the same week Wall Street crashed. [30]

**p4** Almost nobody checked his numbers. When his American bankers asked about audits, they accepted a Swedish one. As Time put it later, they agreed "to accept a Swedish audit of the greatest Swede." [26]

**p5** And control stayed with him. A small class of voting shares, worth about fifty thousand dollars, mostly his, controlled companies valued at more than a billion. [24]

## CHAPTER 5 · The Trick (5:55–7:10)

**r1** Here is the problem with lending to countries. A government pays you back slowly, over decades. But Kreuger's investors expected big dividends every year.

**r2** So, from the mid-twenties, the dividends were largely paid not from profits, but from the investors' own capital: new money coming in, paid out to the people who came before. [23]

**r3** The money moved through a maze of companies: in Liechtenstein, the Netherlands, and shells with tiny capital and huge debts. Each set of books made the others look healthy. [32]

**r4** And when he needed collateral, he made it. In his office, Kreuger forged the signatures of Italian officials on government bonds. Fake bonds worth about a hundred million dollars, printed in Stockholm from plates he delivered himself. [33]

**r5** It worked as long as new money kept arriving. Then the world stopped lending.

## CHAPTER 6 · Out of Matches — 1929–1932 (7:10–8:30)

**c1** After the crash of 1929, investors pulled back. Banks wanted their money. Kreuger borrowed from Swedish banks: about eight hundred million kronor, roughly a tenth of Sweden's entire economy. [35]

**c2** Then came a deal that went wrong. He had sold control of the telephone maker Ericsson to the American company ITT. In early 1932, ITT discovered the numbers he'd shown them weren't true, and demanded eleven million dollars back. He couldn't pay. [36]

**c3** On March 12, 1932, he was found dead in his Paris apartment. He left letters. In one, he wrote that he had made such a mess of things that this was the most satisfactory solution. [37]

**c4** The Paris police held back the news until the markets closed. Officially, it was suicide. His family always insisted he was murdered. That has never been proven. [37,38]

**c5** When the news broke, the Stockholm stock exchange shut its doors. Swedish banks that had lent him money were suddenly in danger, and the government had to step in. [35,46,47]

## CHAPTER 7 · The Books — 1932–1933 (8:30–9:30)

**a1** The accounting firm Price Waterhouse went through his records. Its verdict: the manipulations were so childish that anyone with a basic knowledge of bookkeeping could have seen them. [34]

**a2** Over fourteen years, he had reported more than three hundred million dollars in profits. The real figure: about forty million. Along the way, he had taken about five hundred sixty million dollars from investors, and about one hundred sixty-four million from banks. [41]

**a3** Kreuger and Toll went bankrupt, the largest bankruptcy on record at that time. When the American receiver opened the accounts, it found less than ten thousand dollars in cash. Creditors got back about thirty-two cents on the dollar. [43,44]

**a4** In Sweden, the prime minister resigned after admitting his party had taken Kreuger's money. In Washington, the Senate held hearings. The scandal helped shape America's new securities laws of 1933 and 1934, and the idea that public companies need independent audits. [46,48,49]

## ENDING · The Matches Were Real (9:30–10:00)

**e1** The strangest part? Not everything was fake. The factories were real. His loan to Germany kept paying interest long after he was gone. And Swedish Match survived. In 2022, it was bought for about sixteen billion dollars. [50,51]

**e1b** That's why a modern historian called him the father of today's financial market. A dubious honor. Because what Kreuger really mastered wasn't matches. It was making people believe. [51]

**e2** The matches were real. The money wasn't.
