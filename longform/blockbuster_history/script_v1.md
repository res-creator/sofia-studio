# How Blockbuster Said No to Netflix — English script v1 (draft for the owner's approval)

Narrator: Bryan (ElevenLabs, as in the LEGO film) unless the owner says otherwise. American English. [Brackets] are not read aloud; numbers in brackets = facts in `research.md`.
Length ≈ 8.5 min (36 lines, 6,470 characters; the LEGO film had 8,089 characters = 10:21 with Bryan).
Spine of the film: **the famous story is wrong in one important way.** Blockbuster did not ignore the internet. It saw Netflix coming, fought back and almost won. Then a fight about money inside its own boardroom switched the fix off, and the company that had made its fortune on late fees ran out of time.
Chain: decision → consequence → new problem → next decision. Disputed moments are told as "according to…".

## COLD OPEN — Fifty million dollars (0:00–0:45)

**h1** In the year 2000, three men from a small, money-losing website flew to Dallas to offer their company to the biggest video rental chain on Earth. The price: fifty million dollars. [15,16]

**h2** The chain said no. Ten years later, it was bankrupt. The website was Netflix. Today it is worth more than two hundred billion dollars. [42,49]

**h3** That's the story everyone knows. But it leaves out the strangest part. Blockbuster didn't miss the internet. It saw Netflix coming, fought back, and for a few months it was winning. [27,28]

**h4** So what really killed it? To answer that, you have to start with the thing Blockbuster's customers hated most. And the thing its accountants loved. The late fee.

## CHAPTER 1 · Be Kind, Rewind — 1985–1999 (0:45–2:10)

**k1** The first Blockbuster opened in Dallas, Texas, in October 1985, with thousands of tapes on its shelves. Its founder, David Cook, came from the software business. [1]

**k2** In 1987, the businessman Wayne Huizenga and his partners bought control. Blockbuster had about nineteen stores. Huizenga opened them at a frantic pace. By the end of 1993, there were more than three and a half thousand. [2,4]

**k3** In 1994, the media giant Viacom bought Blockbuster for eight point four billion dollars. [5]

**k4** And underneath all that growth was a quiet money machine. If you brought a tape back late, you paid extra. In 1999, those "extended viewing fees" brought Blockbuster almost seven hundred million dollars. About fifteen cents of every dollar it earned. [11]

**k5** Customers hated them. But every late fee was almost pure profit. Remember that. It decides the rest of this story.

## CHAPTER 2 · The Offer — 1997–2000 (2:10–4:00)

**n1** In 1997, two Silicon Valley entrepreneurs, Reed Hastings and Marc Randolph, started a company to rent movies by mail, on the new DVD discs. They called it Netflix. [13]

**n2** Hastings later said the idea came after he paid a forty-dollar late fee for Apollo 13. Randolph says that's a convenient fiction. Either way, Netflix soon dropped late fees completely: one monthly price, keep a disc as long as you like. [13,14]

**n3** By 2000, Netflix had around three hundred thousand subscribers. That year, it would lose fifty-seven million dollars. Blockbuster had nearly seven thousand seven hundred stores and almost five billion dollars in revenue. [8,15,17]

**n4** So Netflix asked for a meeting in Dallas. According to both founders, the pitch was simple. Blockbuster buys Netflix. Netflix runs Blockbuster's website. Blockbuster runs the stores. The price was fifty million dollars. [15,16]

**n5** In Randolph's telling, Blockbuster's chief executive, John Antioco, was struggling not to laugh. The answer was no. Antioco later said he only stopped by to greet the guests, and that nobody took the idea seriously. [16,18]

**n6** And Blockbuster had a plan of its own. That same year, it signed a twenty-year deal with an energy company that wanted to stream movies over the internet. The company was Enron. The deal fell apart within a year. [22]

## CHAPTER 3 · The Giant — 2001–2004 (4:00–5:10)

**p1** The dot-com crash nearly killed Netflix. In 2001, it laid off about a third of its staff. But it survived, went public in 2002, and by early 2003 it had passed a million subscribers. [20,21]

**p2** Blockbuster, meanwhile, kept growing. By the end of 2004, it had about nine thousand one hundred stores, more than eighty thousand employees, and six billion dollars in revenue. The biggest year it would ever have. [9]

**p3** That year, it also launched its own rent-by-mail website. And its chief executive made two expensive bets. About two hundred million dollars to build the online business. And about two hundred million more to do something unthinkable. [23,25]

## CHAPTER 4 · No More Late Fees — 2005–2007 (5:10–6:50)

**f1** In January 2005, Blockbuster announced "the end of late fees." The fees that had once brought in hundreds of millions of dollars a year were gone. [24,25]

**f2** It wasn't quite that simple. Keep a movie a week too long, and Blockbuster sold it to you. Bring it back, and you paid a restocking fee. Dozens of states said the ads were misleading, and Blockbuster settled. [24,26]

**f3** But the bigger move came in late 2006. It was called Total Access. Rent online, and return the disc in any Blockbuster store, for a free movie on the spot. Netflix had the website. Blockbuster had the website and nine thousand stores. [27]

**f4** It worked. In the first three months of 2007, Total Access added about eight hundred thousand subscribers, to around three million. That spring, Netflix lost subscribers for the first time in its history. [27,28]

**f5** For a moment, the giant had caught the startup. And then the giant turned on itself.

## CHAPTER 5 · The Boardroom — 2005–2008 (6:50–8:20)

**i1** The billionaire investor Carl Icahn had bought a big stake in Blockbuster. He thought Antioco was spending far too much, and paying himself too much. In 2005, Icahn won a fight for seats on the board. [31,32]

**i2** The two men clashed over money: over the cost of Total Access, and over Antioco's bonus. In 2007, Antioco announced he would leave. [33]

**i3** His replacement, Jim Keyes, came from the convenience-store chain 7-Eleven. Total Access was losing money, so he pulled back on it, and put the focus back on the stores. Blockbuster even made an offer of about a billion dollars for the electronics chain Circuit City. Then it withdrew. [34,37]

**i4** Meanwhile, the market moved on. Netflix began streaming movies in 2007. Red kiosks outside supermarkets rented DVDs for a dollar a night. [38,39]

**i5** In late 2008, Keyes was asked about the competition. "Neither RedBox nor Netflix are even on the radar screen," he said. In his view, the real rivals were Walmart and Apple. [35]

**i6** Years later, Icahn admitted he didn't know what would have happened if they'd avoided the fight over the bonus, and Antioco had kept growing Total Access. [33]

## CHAPTER 6 · Closing Time — 2008–2014 (8:20–9:20)

**z1** In 2008, Blockbuster lost three hundred seventy-four million dollars. In 2009, five hundred fifty-eight million. In July 2010, its stock was kicked off the New York Stock Exchange. [40,41]

**z2** In September 2010, Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy, with nearly a billion dollars of debt. [42]

**z3** The next spring, the satellite TV company DISH bought what was left, in a deal valued at about three hundred twenty million dollars. The cash it actually paid: about two hundred twenty-six million. By January 2014, every company-owned store had closed. [43,44]

**z4** Except one. A franchise store in Bend, Oregon, kept its lights on. Today it's known as the last Blockbuster on Earth. People travel there just to rent a movie, the old way. [45]

## CHAPTER 7 · Be Kind — the ending (9:20–10:00)

**e1** So what killed Blockbuster? Not one bad meeting. A business built on late fees, a chain of stores that cost a fortune to run, and a fix that cost money right when the people watching the money took control. [11,25,33,34]

**e2** Netflix, the company Blockbuster could have bought for fifty million dollars, now has more than three hundred twenty-five million paid memberships. [47]

**e3** And in Bend, Oregon, the last Blockbuster still rents movies on DVD. With a due date. [45]
