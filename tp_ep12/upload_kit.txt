PINNED COMMENT
Sources: Rep. Harold Froehlich's (R-Wis.) press release of Dec. 11, 1973 ("The U.S. may face a serious shortage of toilet paper within a few months"), after a federal buying office got fewer bids than usual; Johnny Carson's Tonight Show monologue of Dec. 19, 1973 (about 20 million viewers); stores limited rolls per customer and one raised the price from 39¢ to 69¢ a roll (as reported by the St. Petersburg Times); Scott Paper said unfounded rumors had caused excessive demand; Carson later told viewers there was no shortage. The scare faded by about February 1974. Exact wording of the joke differs between accounts, so the video paraphrases it. The host in the video is a generic drawing, not a portrait.
Next time the shelves go empty: check whether the factories actually stopped, not just whether the shelf did.

YOUTUBE SHORTS
Title: The Toilet Paper Panic of 1973: how one TV joke emptied America's shelves
Description: In December 1973, during the oil crisis, a Wisconsin congressman warned that the U.S. "may" face a toilet paper shortage. On December 19, Johnny Carson joked about a toilet paper shortage on The Tonight Show — without the "may." Within days, shoppers filled their carts, stores set limits and some raised prices, and shelves stayed empty, even though the factories kept running. Carson later told viewers there was no shortage. The panic created the shortage. (Illustrated; the host is a generic drawing.)
