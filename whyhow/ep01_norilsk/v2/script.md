## COLD OPEN (≈ 0:00–0:55)

*Black → wind sound → slow push-in on a Commons photo of Norilsk panel blocks in snow. Counter: −53°C.*

**P001** Winter in the Arctic. The sun hasn't risen for weeks. Outside, the air can drop to minus fifty. Inside the apartment blocks, it's plus twenty. And under every one of those blocks, the ground is frozen solid. [1][4]

*Cut the ground open under the photo: ice-blue layer, piles appear.*

**P002** This is Norilsk. One hundred and seventy-four thousand people, three hundred kilometers north of the Arctic Circle. No road leads here from the rest of the country. [1][2]

**P002B** In twenty twenty, one giant fuel tank here failed, and twenty-one thousand tons of diesel poured into the rivers. Was it the frozen ground? Hold on to that question. We'll come back to it. [17]

**P002C** Frozen ground sounds like a great foundation. It's as hard as concrete. But there's a catch. The moment you build a warm house on top of it, the ice underneath starts to melt. And a house standing on melting ice does exactly what you'd expect. It sinks. It tilts. It cracks.

**P003** So how does Norilsk do it? How do you keep a whole city standing on ice? And what happens when the ice starts losing? To answer that, we need to go down. About ten meters down.

*Title card: WHY DON'T HOUSES IN NORILSK SINK? — channel tag "How It's Run".*

## CHAPTER 1 — A city that shouldn't exist (≈ 0:55–3:00)

*Map flight: globe → Russia → Taymyr → pin "Norilsk 69°N 88°E". Dotted line: no roads.*

**P004** Norilsk sits on the edge of the Taymyr Peninsula in Arctic Siberia, at sixty-nine degrees north. It is the largest city this far north on the planet. Murmansk, in Russia's northwest, is bigger, but it lies further south. [1][3]

**P005** The climate is brutal. The average temperature over a whole year is around minus eight degrees Celsius. In January, the average is almost minus twenty-seven. The record low is minus fifty-three. And for about six weeks every winter, from the end of November until the middle of January, the sun does not rise at all. [1][4]

*Calendar bar: polar night ≈ 30 Nov – 13 Jan.*

**P006** Nobody would build a city here for the view. Norilsk exists because of what lies under the snow. The ground here holds one of the richest deposits of nickel, copper and palladium on Earth. Today, the company that mines it, Nornickel, produces more than forty percent of the world's palladium, a metal used in car exhaust catalysts. [5]

*Counter: >40% of world palladium. Car catalyst icon (our graphic).*

**P007** The city's beginnings are dark. In nineteen thirty-five, the Soviet government opened a labor camp here called Norillag. Over the next twenty-one years, hundreds of thousands of prisoners were sent to dig the mines and build the first factories and streets. Historians estimate around four hundred thousand people passed through the camp. Official records list more than sixteen thousand deaths, and the real number is believed to be higher. [6]

*Commons archive photo (PD) of Norillag era, slow move. Text: NORILLAG 1935–1956.*

**P008** Those prisoners were the first to discover the problem we're talking about today. Because when they built their first stone houses, the ground underneath simply refused to cooperate. To see why, we need to know what permafrost really is.

---

## CHAPTER 2 — What is permafrost, really? (≈ 3:00–5:00)

*Cross-section animation: surface → active layer (brown) → permafrost (blue) with ice lenses.*

**P009** Permafrost isn't a special kind of ice. It's a definition. Any ground that stays at or below zero degrees Celsius for at least two years in a row counts as permafrost. It can be rock, sand, clay or peat. What matters is the temperature. [7]

**P010** The permafrost around Norilsk is continuous. That means it isn't patchy. It's everywhere, under every street, every factory and every apartment block. [8]

**P011** But the very top of the ground behaves differently. Every summer, the upper layer thaws, and every winter it freezes again. Scientists call it the active layer. Near Norilsk, it is about one meter thick. Below it, the ground stays frozen all year round. [8][9]

*Active layer pulses: thaw in summer, freeze in winter. Label "≈ 1 m".*

**P012** Here's why that frozen layer matters so much. In permafrost, ice works like glue. It fills the gaps between grains of sand and clay and locks them together. Frozen clay can carry enormous weight. Thawed clay, full of melted water, turns into something closer to mud.

*Split screen: frozen block holds a weight → thawed block slumps.*

**P013** Now, put a building on top of it. A heated building, in a place where it's minus thirty outside and plus twenty inside. Heat flows down through the floor, year after year. The ground under the building warms, the ice melts, and the soil loses its strength. One side settles faster than the other. The walls crack. That is exactly what happened to the first stone houses of Norilsk.

## CHAPTER 3 — The houses that cracked (≈ 5:00–6:15)

*Our drawing of a low stone house, crack line animating across the wall.*

**P014** Between nineteen thirty-seven and nineteen forty, around thirty low stone houses were built in Norilsk on shallow foundations, the way you'd build almost anywhere else. According to Russian engineering studies, about half of them began to deform almost immediately. [10]

**P015** The ground was doing what frozen ground does. As soon as it was warmed from above, it gave way. For the next fifteen years or so, builders tried to solve this by digging down to solid rock and standing buildings on columns that reached it. That works, but only where rock is close to the surface, and it's slow and expensive. [10]

**P016** A city of tens of thousands couldn't grow like that. Soviet engineers needed a different idea. And the idea they settled on sounds almost backwards. Instead of fighting the frozen ground, they decided to protect it. And the way they did it is hiding in plain sight, under every building in the city.

---

## CHAPTER 4 — The trick: stilts and cold air (≈ 6:15–9:00)

*Chapter card: "4. Keep it frozen". Then a big cross-section of a five-storey block on piles.*

**P017** Soviet building codes describe two ways of building on permafrost. Principle Two accepts that the ground will thaw and tries to design for it. Principle One does the opposite. It says: keep the ground frozen for the whole life of the building. Most of Norilsk was built on Principle One. [11][8]

**P018** Here's how it works. First, long piles are driven or drilled deep into the permafrost, well below the active layer. As the ground around them refreezes, the piles become frozen in place, gripped by the ice along their whole length. Think of a stick frozen into an ice cube: you can't pull it out. The building's weight is carried by that frozen grip. [12]

*Piles slide down into the blue layer; frost crystals form around them.*

**P019** Second, and this is the clever part, the building doesn't touch the ground. It stands on those piles above an open, ventilated gap between the soil and the first floor. Look at almost any old apartment block in Norilsk, and you'll see it: the whole building seems to hover above the snow. [12]

*Commons photo of a Norilsk block with visible open ground floor / crawl space. Arrow: "ventilated space".*

**P020** That gap is the real foundation. It turns the space under the house into a fridge for the ground. In winter, freezing air blows through it and pulls heat out of the ground, so the permafrost gets even colder. In summer, the building's shadow keeps the sun off the soil. The heat from the apartments escapes into the air instead of into the ground. [12]

*Animation: blue winter wind through the gap; heat arrows go sideways, not down.*

**P021** It's the same reason you'll see something else everywhere in the city: pipes running above ground. Hot water and heating pipes buried in the soil would act like a giant heater under the streets. So they're lifted up, wrapped, and kept away from the frozen ground. [1]

**P022** Put together, it's a simple deal. The city keeps the ground frozen, and the frozen ground holds the city up. It worked well enough that Norilsk grew into a city of panel blocks, five and nine storeys high, most of them built before the nineteen eighties. [8]

**P023** But this deal has a weak point. It only works if the ground stays cold. And in Norilsk, the ground has been getting warmer, from two directions at once. One of them comes from the city itself.

---

## CHAPTER 5 — When the trick fails (≈ 9:00–11:15)

*Chapter card: "5. The ground starts losing".*

**P024** The first problem is the city itself. That ventilated gap only works if air can move through it. When snow piles up against the building, it acts like a blanket and keeps the ground warm. When the gap gets blocked, or when a leaking hot-water or sewage pipe floods the space under a building, the ice below starts to melt from the top. [8][13]

*Animation: snowdrift closes the gap; a pipe drips; a red warm pocket grows under the house.*

**P025** Researchers who measured ground temperatures in Norilsk found that at ten meters deep, the permafrost used to be between minus seven and minus half a degree before mass construction began. By the two thousands, in parts of the city, it was between minus two and a half and plus half a degree. In some spots, the ground had warmed by three to five degrees compared with the nineteen forties. Some pockets weren't frozen at all anymore. [8]

*Thermometer at −10 m: from −7…−0.5 → −2.5…+0.5 °C.*

**P026** The second problem is the climate. Between the nineteen seventies and the twenty tens, the average air temperature in Norilsk rose by one point four degrees. That's a lot for a place where the whole system depends on staying below zero. And the Arctic as a whole has been warming almost four times faster than the rest of the planet since nineteen seventy-nine. [8][14]

*Chart: Arctic vs global warming 1979–2021.*

**P027** When frozen ground warms, even before it melts, it gets weaker. The ice grip on the piles loosens. A study by American and Russian scientists estimated that for Norilsk buildings from the nineteen sixties, the ground's ability to carry their weight had already dropped by about fifteen percent by twenty thirteen. For buildings from the seventies and eighties, by about twenty-one percent. [8]

*Bar chart: bearing capacity −15% (1960s buildings), −21% (1970s–80s buildings).*

**P028** The same team modeled what comes next. By twenty twenty, they projected a drop of more than a third, larger than the safety margins most of those buildings were designed with. By twenty fifty, older foundations could lose around two thirds of their strength. These are model estimates with a wide range of uncertainty, but they all point the same way. [8]

**P029** And the damage is not just on paper. By the mid twenty tens, around three hundred structures in Norilsk had significant deformations. More than a hundred residential buildings were in a state of structural failure, and about five thousand families were still living in them. And almost fifty buildings, five and nine storeys tall, built from the sixties to the eighties, had already been taken apart. [8]

*Commons photos of cracked façades; counter: 300 / 100+ / ~50.*

**P030** You may have seen a scarier number online: that sixty percent of the buildings in Norilsk are deformed. That figure comes from a single statement by a regional emergency official in twenty sixteen, and nobody published how it was counted. Permafrost scientists who surveyed the city gave a lower figure: around thirty-five percent of buildings in Norilsk and Talnakh showing deformation. Lower, but still about one in three. And then, in twenty twenty, came the fuel tank. [15][16]

*Myth card: "60%?" crossed → "≈ 35% (survey)".*

## CHAPTER 6 — May twenty-ninth, twenty twenty (≈ 11:15–13:00)

*Sentinel-2 image of the red-stained Ambarnaya river (Copernicus, June 2020). Date stamp.*

**P031** On the twenty-ninth of May, twenty twenty, a huge fuel tank at a power plant in Norilsk failed. About twenty-one thousand tons of diesel poured out, into the soil and into local rivers. It was one of the worst oil spills ever recorded in the Arctic. [17]

**P032** At first, the company that owned the plant, a subsidiary of Nornickel, pointed to the permafrost. The supports under the tank, it said, had sunk because the ground had thawed. That story made headlines around the world: the first big disaster caused by melting permafrost. [17][18]

**P033** But the investigations told a more complicated story. An engineering review commissioned by Nornickel found that some of the tank's piles were shorter than designed and had never been set into the bedrock. Russia's industrial safety regulator went further. It found no sign of thawed permafrost under the tank, and blamed weak foundations and missing inspections instead. The tank had been out of service for repairs and had gone back into use in twenty nineteen without being properly checked. [18][19]

*Two cards side by side: COMPANY: "thaw" · REGULATOR: "foundations + maintenance".*

**P034** In twenty twenty-one, a Russian court ordered Nornickel to pay one hundred and forty-six billion rubles, nearly two billion US dollars, for the environmental damage. The company paid it in March of that year. It was the largest environmental fine in Russia's history. [20]

**P035** So was it the permafrost, or was it people? The honest answer is that it doesn't really matter which. A building on frozen ground only stands as long as two things hold: the ground stays cold, and humans do their job. In Norilsk, both are under pressure. So what can a city do when its ground is getting warmer? It can fight back, with a pipe that needs no power at all.

---

## CHAPTER 7 — Freezing the ground on purpose (≈ 13:00–15:00)

*Chapter card: "7. A refrigerator under the city". Animation of a thermosiphon: pipe, liquid at bottom, vapor rising, radiator top.*

**P036** If the ground is getting too warm on its own, the answer is to cool it down. And engineers have a device that does exactly that, with no electricity at all. It's called a thermosiphon, and it's one of the smartest bits of engineering you've never noticed.

**P037** It's a sealed metal pipe, pushed deep into the ground, with a little liquid inside, often ammonia or carbon dioxide. In winter, the top of the pipe is in freezing air. The liquid at the bottom picks up heat from the soil, turns into vapor and rises. At the cold top, the vapor gives its heat away to the air, turns back into liquid, and runs down again. Over and over, all winter long. [21]

*Loop animation: blue liquid down, orange vapor up; ground around the pipe turns deeper blue.*

**P038** In summer, when the air is warmer than the ground, the cycle simply stops. So the pipe only ever moves heat in one direction: out of the ground. Every winter, it stores up extra cold for the summer ahead.

**P039** You've probably seen thermosiphons without knowing it. The Trans-Alaska Pipeline is held up by more than a hundred and twenty thousand of them. The Qinghai–Tibet railway, which crosses about five hundred and fifty kilometers of permafrost, uses them together with embankments of crushed rock that let cold air in. [21][22]

*Commons photos: Alaska pipeline supports with radiator fins; Qinghai–Tibet railway embankment.*

**P040** Norilsk is now doing the same. After pilot projects on two apartment buildings in twenty nineteen and twenty twenty, the authorities began installing soil cooling systems under more homes. In twenty twenty-four, Russia's ministry for the Far East and the Arctic said hundreds of houses in Norilsk would get them by twenty thirty-five. [23]

**P041** At the same time, the city is being partly rebuilt. Under a renovation program agreed in twenty twenty-one between the government and Nornickel, dozens of failing buildings are planned to be demolished and replaced with new apartment blocks. And Nornickel has put around a hundred and sixty-five buildings and structures under monitoring, with sensors that track the ground temperature and every tiny movement. [24][25]

*Map of Norilsk with dots lighting up: monitored buildings.*

## ENDING — The answer (≈ 15:00–16:00)

*Pull back from the cross-section to the city in polar night, windows lit.*

**P042** So, why don't the houses in Norilsk sink into the permafrost? Because the city was built on a promise to the ground: we will keep you frozen. Stilts lift the buildings into the wind, pipes stay above the surface, and now, more and more, steel tubes quietly pump the heat out of the earth every winter.

**P043** But here's the twist. Norilsk doesn't really stand on frozen ground. It stands on ground it keeps frozen. The city works like a giant refrigerator turned upside down, cooling the earth beneath its own feet. And the warmer the Arctic gets, the harder that refrigerator has to work.

**P044** Norilsk isn't alone. Scientists estimate that by twenty fifty, nearly four million people and around seventy percent of the buildings, roads and pipelines in the Arctic's permafrost zone will sit in areas where the ground is likely to thaw. Norilsk is just where the problem shows up first. [26]

**P044B** So tonight, somewhere in Norilsk, it's minus thirty outside and plus twenty inside. And ten meters below the floor, the ground is still frozen, because the whole city is working to keep it that way.

**P045** So here's my question for you. Would you live in a city where the ground under your house has to be refrigerated to keep it standing? Tell me in the comments, and tell me which place on Earth we should explain next. This was How It's Run.
