# EP20 — Key West Has No Rivers or Lakes. So How Do 25,000 People Drink?

## COLD OPEN

**A001** Key West has no rivers. No lakes. And almost no fresh water underground. [1]

**A002** And yet about twenty-five thousand people live here, and every one of them can turn on a tap. [2]

**A003** So where does the water come from?

**A004** Here's the wild part. It comes from a hundred and thirty miles away, through one pipe, hopping across the ocean on dozens of bridges. [3]

**A005** Your first guess might be: they just collect the rain. Key West does get about three feet of rain a year. But a normal roof catches nowhere near enough for a modern family. [4]

**A006** Second guess: they turn seawater into drinking water. They can! But most days, those machines are switched off. [5]

**A007** So how does a tiny island at the very end of the road keep its showers running? We'll go back to the days of rain barrels, ride a railroad across the sea, follow the pipe, survive a few hurricanes, and find out what happens after you flush. Let's go!

---

## CH1 — Every drop from the sky

**A008** For most of its history, Key West drank the sky.

**A009** Every house had a cistern: a big tank under the house or right next to it. When it rained, water ran off the roof, through the gutters, and into the tank. That was your drinking water for the dry months. [6]

**A010** Some families even floated a thin film of kerosene on top, to stop mosquitoes breeding in it. Yum. [6]

**A011** And people got clever. Brackish water from shallow wells went into the toilets. Salt water fought the fires. Fresh rainwater was way too precious for that. [6]

**A011B** And do the math. A thousand-square-foot roof in the Keys catches around twenty-two thousand gallons in a year. Even a small, careful household needs more than that. So even a perfect cistern could run dry. [6]

**A012** In the eighteen-sixties, the Army even shipped in a high-tech British machine to boil seawater into fresh water at Fort Taylor. It made about seven thousand gallons a day. [7]

**A013** That's about what twenty American homes use today. For a whole fort. [7][8]

**A014** And when the rain didn't come? Water had to arrive by boat, in big tanks, from the mainland. Which was fine, until the town kept growing.

---

## CH2 — Water by train

**A015** Then came one of the craziest building projects in American history.

**A016** Henry Flagler, an oil tycoon who'd already built hotels and railroads all down Florida, decided to run a railroad across the sea, island to island, all the way to Key West. [9]

**A017** Building it in the middle of the ocean had one big problem. Thousands of workers, tons of concrete, and almost no fresh water. His crews drilled wells all along the Keys. Most of them hit salt water. [6]

**A018** So the railroad hauled water in. Tank cars full of it, rolling out over the sea from the mainland, millions of gallons for the workers and for the concrete. [6]

**A019** In January nineteen twelve, the first train rolled into Key West, and the crowds went wild. For the first time, the island was tied to the mainland. [9]

**A020** But not for long. On Labor Day, nineteen thirty-five, the most intense hurricane ever to hit the United States tore through the Upper Keys. It swept away miles of track. [10]

**A021** The railroad was never rebuilt. The water trains stopped. And the old rail bridges were turned into a road: the Overseas Highway. [10]

---

## CH3 — The longest straw

**A022** So who finally fixed Key West's water? The Navy.

**A023** When the Navy base reopened before World War Two, it needed about seventy-five thousand gallons a day. Its own little seawater plant made thirty-five thousand. Not even half. [6]

**A024** So the Navy did something bold. It paid two-thirds of the bill for a pipeline. A hundred and twenty-eight miles of eighteen-inch steel pipe, from wells in Florida City on the mainland, all the way down the Keys to Key West. [6]

**A025** In some places the pipe was buried. At thirteen channels it went underwater. Everywhere else, it simply rode along the bridges. [6]

**A026** On September twenty-second, nineteen forty-two, the first pipeline water reached Key West. [6]

**A027** And here's a fun detail: a single drop took about six days to make the whole trip. [6]

**A027B** That first pipe held about ten million gallons at any moment. Picture fifteen Olympic swimming pools, squeezed into a skinny tube a hundred and twenty-eight miles long. [6][18]

**A028** But eighteen inches wasn't enough for long. By the fifties, so many people had moved down that on weekends, the water pressure basically vanished. If you lived on a second floor, good luck taking a shower. [6]

**A028B** In nineteen seventy-six, the Navy handed the whole thing over. A new local agency, the Florida Keys Aqueduct Authority, bought the wells, the pumps and the pipe for seven million dollars. [6]

**A029** In nineteen eighty, the state broke ground on a much bigger pipe, up to thirty-six inches wide. It was finished in nineteen eighty-two. [6]

**A030** And that's still the lifeline today. The water starts as rain soaking into a layer of porous limestone under South Florida, called the Biscayne Aquifer. Wells in Florida City pump it up, a plant cleans it, and big pumps push it south. [11][6]

**A031** About a hundred and thirty miles of pipe. Forty-five bridge crossings. Around twenty million gallons a day, for about fifty-five thousand customers all along the Keys. [3]

**A032** Basically, the Florida Keys drink through one very, very long straw.

**A032B** And if you drive the Overseas Highway, look beside some of the bridges. That big pipe running next to you, over the open water? That's somebody's morning shower, on its way south. [3]

---

## CH4 — When the storm comes

**A033** Now, here's the scary question. What happens to one long straw in hurricane country?

**A034** In nineteen sixty, Hurricane Donna hit the Keys. Bridges were battered, and the water pipe broke in several places. It was the first time the whole system really failed. [6][12]

**A035** So the Keys built a backup. In nineteen sixty-seven, the Navy opened a seawater desalination plant on Stock Island, right next to Key West. [6]

**A036** Then came September twenty seventeen. Hurricane Irma, a Category Four, smashed into the Lower Keys. [13]

**A037** Trees were ripped out of the ground, and their roots tore up water lines with them. The water stopped flowing down from Florida City, and the whole chain was told to boil their water. [13][14]

**A038** That's when the backup plants switched on. Machines at Stock Island and Marathon started turning seawater into drinking water. [13]

**A039** The head of the water authority, Kirk Zuelch, put it simply: without that plant, Key West couldn't have reopened as soon as it did. It was a real lifeline. [13]

**A040** But Irma damaged that old plant too. So in May twenty twenty-five, a brand-new one opened on Stock Island. It's built up high and hardened for storms, and it can make about four million gallons a day. [5]

**A041** Normally it just waits. It only switches on in emergencies, or when demand peaks. Like a spare tire for a whole city. [5]

---

## CH5 — Where it all goes

**A042** Okay. The water made it all the way down. You took your shower. Now what? Where does it go?

**A043** For decades, the answer in much of the Keys was: into the ground. Septic tanks and cesspits, sitting in porous limestone, right next to the ocean. [15]

**A044** And just offshore is the only living coral barrier reef in the continental United States. Leaking sewage feeds algae, and algae is bad news for coral. [15][16]

**A045** So the state ordered a huge clean-up. Tens of thousands of septic tanks and cesspits were removed, and today about ninety-four percent of homes in Monroe County are hooked up to central sewers with advanced treatment. [15]

**A046** And the pipe itself is getting old. Saltwater and tides eat at buried steel. So the water authority is replacing the entire line, section by section, over the next twenty to thirty years. [3]

**A047** The price? Roughly ten million dollars a mile. The first four miles alone, in Islamorada, cost about forty-two million. And at some bridges, the new pipe goes deep under the seabed, safe from storms. [3][17]

**A047B** And the straw is only getting busier. Key West alone welcomed more than half a million cruise passengers in twenty twenty-four. Every hotel room, every restaurant, every ice machine drinks from the same pipe. [19]

**A049** The water in your hotel shower fell as rain over the Everglades, soaked into the rock, got pumped up in Florida City, and then traveled a hundred and thirty miles south, across the sea, on forty-five bridges, just to reach you.

**A050** For more than eighty years, one long pipe has kept the end of the road alive.
