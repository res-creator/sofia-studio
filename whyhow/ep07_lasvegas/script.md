# EP07 — Las Vegas Shouldn't Exist. Here's How It Does.

Format: How It's Run, English, voice am_puck speed 1.08 (~190 wpm), target ~10 min. Mostly real footage of Las Vegas, the Strip, Lake Mead, Hoover Dam, the desert; archive photos (1905 auction, 1950s atomic tests — US government footage, public domain); cut every 2–4 s; grid graphics as short inserts.
Rule for this episode: skip the facts everyone repeats (no clocks in casinos, "what happens in Vegas", etc.). Only the lesser-known story of how the city survives.
Numbers in [brackets] point to SOURCES.md.

## COLD OPEN

**L001** Here's something most visitors never find out. The famous Las Vegas Strip, the casinos, the fountains, the giant hotels, isn't actually in the city of Las Vegas. [1]

**L002** The city drinks most of its water twice. In the nineteen fifties, its hotels threw parties for nuclear explosions. And right now, under its streets, hundreds of people are living in the dark. [2][5][7]

**L003** This is a city of more than two million people, in a desert that gets about four inches of rain a year. By every rule, it shouldn't exist. [3]

**L004** So how does Las Vegas really work? And what keeps it alive?

---

## CH1 — The meadows

**L005** Let's start with the name. Las Vegas is Spanish for "the meadows." [4]

**L006** Because two hundred years ago, in the middle of this desert, there was grass. Springs came up out of the ground here, and turned a patch of the valley green. Travelers crossing the Mojave stopped here for water. [4]

**L007** In nineteen-oh-five, a railroad came through, and the railroad company decided to sell the land around its station. On the fifteenth of May, it held an auction, in heat of about a hundred and ten degrees. That auction is considered the birthday of the city. [8]

**L008** The new town drilled wells, and many of them were artesian: the water shot out of the ground by itself. So people simply let it run. Many wells were never capped, and water poured across the desert, day and night. [4]

**L009** You can guess how that ended. The underground water dropped, and in nineteen sixty-two, the springs that gave Las Vegas its name stopped flowing. The meadows were gone. [4]

---

## CH2 — The Strip that escaped the city

**L010** Meanwhile, a few miles south of downtown, along the highway to Los Angeles, a new kind of business was growing: big resort casinos, built just outside the city limits. [1]

**L011** In nineteen fifty, the mayor of Las Vegas wanted to bring them into the city, so he could tax them. The casino bosses didn't like that at all. [1]

**L012** So they went to the county and asked it to create a separate town around their casinos. Once that town existed, the city couldn't annex it. The man leading the campaign was Gus Greenbaum, who ran the Flamingo, a casino opened by the gangster Bugsy Siegel. [1]

**L013** The county agreed, in December nineteen fifty. The new town was called Paradise. And to this day, most of the Strip is in Paradise, run by the county, not by the city of Las Vegas. [1]

---

## CH3 — Bombs as a tourist attraction

**L014** Just a month later, Las Vegas got a very strange new neighbor.

**L015** In January nineteen fifty-one, the United States started testing nuclear bombs at a site in the desert, about sixty-five miles from the city. [5]

**L016** Many tests happened just before sunrise. And on clear mornings, people in Las Vegas could see the flash light up the sky. [5]

**L017** Instead of panicking, Las Vegas turned it into a show. Hotels with views to the north held dawn bomb parties. Guests drank and danced all night, then went up to the roof to watch the explosion. Bars served atomic cocktails. A showgirl at the Sands was even crowned Miss Atomic Bomb. [5]

**L018** But the clouds didn't stay in the desert. The fallout drifted east, onto towns in Nevada and Utah, and many people living downwind later got sick. The tests above ground finally ended in nineteen sixty-two. [5]

---

## CH4 — Drinking the same water twice

**L019** So where does Las Vegas get its water today? Not from the springs. About ninety percent of it comes from one place: Lake Mead, the giant reservoir behind the Hoover Dam, filled by the Colorado River. [2]

**L019B** And Las Vegas got the smallest share of all. In nineteen twenty-eight, when the river's water was divided between the states, Las Vegas was a railroad town of a few thousand people. So Nevada got just three hundred thousand acre-feet a year. That's less than two percent of the river. California got almost fifteen times more. [13]

**L020** And here's the clever part. Almost all the water that goes down a drain indoors in Las Vegas, in showers, sinks, toilets and hotel kitchens, is cleaned and sent back into Lake Mead. [2]

**L021** For every gallon it returns, the city is allowed to take another gallon out. So the water used indoors is used, cleaned and taken out again. That's why the hotels, with about a hundred and fifty thousand rooms, aren't really the problem. [2]

**L022** The real problem is outside. Water sprayed on grass doesn't come back. It soaks into the ground, or evaporates into the desert air. [2]

**L023** So Las Vegas declared war on lawns. The water authority pays people to tear out their grass and replace it with desert plants. And Nevada passed a law: from twenty twenty-seven, decorative grass, the kind nobody walks on, can't be watered with Colorado River water. [2]

**L023B** And it doesn't stop there. New swimming pools at homes can't be bigger than six hundred square feet. And the water authority has water waste investigators, who look for sprinklers spraying onto sidewalks and water running down the street. Ignore their warning, and you get a fine that doubles every time. [14]

**L024** It works. Since two thousand and two, the area has gained almost nine hundred thousand people. And yet, each resident now uses fifty-eight percent less water. [9]

**L025** And the famous fountains of the Bellagio? They don't use city water. Their lake is filled mainly from an old private well, that once watered a golf course on the same spot. [10]

---

## CH5 — The third straw

**L026** But even recycled water can't save a city if the lake itself disappears.

**L027** Since the year two thousand, after years of drought on the Colorado River, the surface of Lake Mead has dropped by more than a hundred and fifty feet. White rings on the rocks show where the water used to be. [9]

**L028** Las Vegas pulls its water out of the lake through intake pipes, nicknamed straws. And as the lake fell, the city realized that one day, its straws could be left sucking air. [6]

**L029** So it built a third one, deeper than the others. Workers dug a tunnel under the lake bed and connected it to the bottom of the lake. Then they built a pumping station that can reach water even if the lake drops to "dead pool," the level where the Hoover Dam can no longer release water downstream. [6]

**L030** It cost more than a billion dollars. In twenty twenty-two, when one of the old intakes stopped working because the water was too low, the new pumps were switched on. [6]

**L031** As the water fell, the lake gave up its secrets. The ruins of St. Thomas, a small town flooded in the nineteen thirties when the lake was filled, came back out of the water, and people could walk through its streets again. [11]

**L032** And here's one more surprise. The Hoover Dam is just thirty miles from Las Vegas, but most of its electricity doesn't go to Nevada. More than half of it goes to California. Nevada gets less than a quarter. [12]

**L032B** The men who built that dam in the nineteen thirties weren't trusted anywhere near Las Vegas. The government built a whole new town for them, Boulder City, and banned gambling there, so the workers wouldn't lose their pay and the dam would stay on schedule. [15]

**L032C** The ban is still in place. Boulder City is one of only two places in Nevada where gambling is illegal. And just outside its border, a casino opened in nineteen thirty-one to catch the workers on their way home. It's still open today. [15]

**L032D** And now the fight over the river has reached the courts. In twenty twenty-six, a new federal plan for the shrinking Colorado River could cut Nevada's small share by more than two-thirds in the worst case. Nevada sued the federal government. [13]

---

## CH6 — The city under the city

**L033** There's one more danger in the desert, and it's not drought. It's the opposite.

**L034** When a summer storm hits Las Vegas, the ground is so hard and dry that the rain doesn't soak in. In minutes, streets can turn into rivers.

**L035** So underneath the city, engineers built a huge network of flood channels and tunnels, about six hundred miles of them, to carry storm water away to Lake Mead. [7]

**L036** Most of the time, those tunnels are dry, dark and cool. And that's why people live in them. Outreach workers estimate that between twelve hundred and fifteen hundred homeless people may be living in the tunnels and washes under the city, some of them right under the Strip. [7]

**L037** They build rooms down there, with beds, furniture and even books. But when a storm comes, the tunnels fill with water very fast. People have died in these floods. [7]

## ENDING

**L038** So, how does Las Vegas exist in the middle of a desert?

**L039** It drained its own springs, and moved on to a river. It drinks most of its water twice, and pays people to tear out their lawns. It dug a straw under a shrinking lake, and built six hundred miles of tunnels for rain that almost never comes.

**L040** And the Strip, the thing everyone thinks of as Las Vegas, isn't even in Las Vegas.
