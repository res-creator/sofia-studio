## COLD OPEN

**A001** June seventh, nineteen forty-two. It's Sunday on Attu, the last island at the far western end of America. The villagers are in church when Japanese soldiers arrive. [25][26]

**A002** Within months, the village is gone. About forty of its people are shipped to Japan. About half of them never come back. [27][28]

**A003** Today, Attu has nobody at all. And it's not the only one. Adak, a few hundred miles east, was a Navy town of thousands. Last year, local news counted only a few dozen people there in winter. [46][52]

**A004** So why do America's Aleutian Islands keep emptying?

**A005** The first answer seems obvious: the war. But Adak grew after the war, to more than four and a half thousand people. Only then did it empty. [42][46]

**A006** The second answer: the weather. Wind almost every day, cloud most of the time. But people lived on these islands for nine thousand years, in the same storms. [5][13]

**A007** So it's something else. To find it, we'll first try to get there. Then we'll go back to nineteen forty-two, to the village that never came home. Then we'll visit the Navy town that turned off the lights. And at the end, we'll see what really empties these islands, and why it's starting to change right now.

## CH1 — The end of America

**A008** The Aleutians run from the tip of the Alaska Peninsula far out into the Pacific, for more than a thousand miles. Laid along the East Coast, they would reach from New York to Miami. [1]

**A009** They are a chain of volcanoes. One of them, Great Sitkin, next door to Adak, has been oozing lava since twenty twenty-one. [3][4]

**A011** The last island, Attu, is fifteen hundred miles from Anchorage. That's London to Moscow. Russia's Kamchatka is less than five hundred and fifty miles away. Nearly three times closer to Russia than to Anchorage. [10]

**A012** Getting there is the first test. Adak has two flights a week from Anchorage. The state ferry turns around at Dutch Harbor, about once a month in summer. West of Adak, there is no scheduled service at all. [11][12]

**A013** And the weather sets the price. On Amchitka, in the middle of the chain, the wind averages twenty-two to thirty miles an hour, and it is calm less than one percent of the time. Adak's airport sign once welcomed visitors to the Birthplace of the Winds, until the wind blew half the sign away. [5][6]

**A014** People here have a word for the worst of it: williwaw. A sudden blast of cold air that falls off the mountains onto the sea. [8]

**A014B** But wind like that never emptied these islands. Something else did. It arrived by ship, in seventeen forty-five.

## CH2 — Nine thousand years without help

**A015** And yet, people lived here long before any plane or ferry. The oldest site in the chain is about nine thousand years old. [13]

**A016** The Unangan, the people the Russians called Aleuts, hunted seals and sea otters from kayaks of sealskin stretched over driftwood and bone. They lived in houses dug halfway into the ground, out of the wind. Many still use the name Aleut today. [15]

**A017** Before outsiders came, there were maybe fifteen to eighteen thousand of them. They needed nothing from anywhere else. The islands fed them. [14][16]

**A018** That changed in seventeen forty-five, when Russian fur hunters landed on Attu. Within weeks, they had killed fifteen men. A bay on Attu is still called Massacre Bay. [17]

**A019** The hunters wanted sea otter fur, and they forced the Unangan to hunt it for them. Violence, hunger and new diseases followed. By the end of Russian rule, the Unangan had lost nearly eighty percent of their people. [19][20]

**A020** And from then on, the islands lived on ships. In eighteen eleven, a visitor found the company post on Attu failing. No ship had stopped there for five years. [22]

**A020B** In eighteen thirty-one, a priest named Netsvetov counted a hundred and twenty people on Attu. [23]

**A021** After America bought Alaska in eighteen sixty-seven, services to the remote islands shrank. Within about ten years, Attu's population halved, from two hundred and twenty to a hundred and seven. [23][24]

**A022** By the summer of nineteen forty-two, forty-five Unangan were left on Attu. That summer, the war reached them from both sides. [23]

## CH3 — The village that never came home

**A023** In June nineteen forty-two, Japanese carrier planes bombed Dutch Harbor. Days later, Japanese troops landed on Kiska and on Attu. [25]

**A024** On Attu, the teacher's husband, Charles Foster Jones, radioed the weather to Dutch Harbor every day. He died within a day of the landing. The Japanese said it was suicide. When his body was dug up in nineteen forty-eight, he had been shot through the head. [26]

**A025** In September, the villagers were loaded into the hold of a coal ship and taken to Otaru, in Japan. Among them was six-year-old Nick Golodoff. [27][63]

**A026** In Japan, they worked and they starved. A mother named Olean Golodoff fed her children orange peels to stop them crying. She lost her husband and three of her seven children there. About half of the Attuans died in Japan. [28][64]

**A027** Meanwhile, the U.S. government removed its own citizens. Eight hundred and eighty-one Unangan from nine villages were taken off the islands. On St. Paul, people got a few hours' notice, and one suitcase each. [30]

**A027B** Greg Fratis was two. Years later, he remembered the elders' words: one suitcase, the boat is here. At seventy-seven, he said: let's not let it happen again. [66]

**A028** On Atka, American servicemen set the homes and the church on fire, so the Japanese couldn't use them. [31]

**A029** The evacuees were put in an old cannery, a herring plant and an abandoned mine camp in Southeast Alaska, with rotten floors and little medical care. At least seventy-four people died in the camps. [32][33]

**A034** But the strangest battle out here was still to come. And it was won by cutting a supply line.

## CH4 — The battle for an empty island

**A035** In March nineteen forty-three, American ships fought a four-hour gun battle near the Komandorski Islands and turned back a Japanese supply convoy. After that, Japan never again sent surface ships to supply the Aleutians. [37]

**A036** Two months later, fifteen thousand American soldiers landed on Attu in fog. The fight ended in one of the largest banzai charges of the war. Of about twenty-five hundred Japanese, only twenty-nine survived. About five hundred and fifty Americans died, and twelve hundred more were hurt by the cold. [38]

**A036B** Japan is still bringing its dead home. In twenty twenty-four, a team dug up two more soldiers. Fog and snow leave only July and August to dig. [55]

**A037** Then came Kiska. In August, more than thirty-four thousand American and Canadian troops stormed the island. [39]

**A038** It was empty. Cut off from supplies, the Japanese had slipped away in the fog more than two weeks earlier. [39]

**A039** Even so, about three hundred Allied men were killed or wounded there. By friendly fire in the fog, by booby traps, and by a mine that tore open the destroyer Abner Read. [40]

**A030** After the battles, the evacuees finally came home. Many found their houses and churches looted by American troops. [34]

**A031** And four villages never came back at all: Attu, Biorka, Kashega and Makushin. The government decided they were too costly to travel to. [35]

**A032** The surviving Attuans were sent to Atka instead. Nick Golodoff lived there for the rest of his life. In nineteen eighty-eight, Congress paid each surviving evacuee twelve thousand dollars. [29][36][63]

## CH5 — The town that turned off the lights

**A040** After the war, the military stayed. And Adak became one of the strangest towns in America. [42]

**A041** In nineteen ninety, the Navy base had more than four and a half thousand people, and at its peak nearly six thousand. It had a McDonald's, a Pizza Hut, a movie theater, even a ski area. [42][43]

**A042** It even had a national forest. During the war, the story goes, soldiers planted a few trees on the treeless island, to lift morale. Most of them died. Someone put up a sign: You are now entering and leaving the Adak National Forest. [44]

**A043** Then the Cold War ended. In nineteen ninety-seven, the base closed. [45]

**A044** By two thousand, Adak had three hundred and sixteen people. By twenty twenty, about a hundred and seventy. A ninety-six percent drop. If New York shrank like that, it would be a city of three hundred thousand. [46]

**A045** Buildings made for thousands now stand empty. In one recent school year, Adak's school had five students. [47][48]

**A046** The town tried to live on fish. Its plant once had up to three hundred jobs, and it depended on a federal cod quota. In twenty nineteen, a court struck the quota down. The plant closed the next year. [49]

**A047** Then, in October twenty twenty-five, Adak's power failed. Its only backup generator broke. The replacement was on a barge in the Bering Sea. To keep the school and clinic running, the town borrowed a generator from its own closed fish plant. For seventeen days, parts of the town lived without electricity. [50]

**A048** One resident, Steven Carroll, ran his own generator and cooked for three families. "I don't know how long we can get by," he said. [67]

**A049** And Attu? In twenty ten, the last twenty-one people on the island, all Coast Guard, left when their navigation station closed. Even the closing ceremony ran a day late: fog kept the plane away. [52]

## CH6 — What really empties the islands

**A050** So what empties the Aleutians?

**A051** Not the weather alone. For nine thousand years, people lived here on what the sea gave them. [13][16]

**A052** But since seventeen forty-five, the islands have lived on a line from outside: fur ships, government services, military money, a fish quota, a barge with a generator. And the first ships didn't just supply the islands. They brought the collapse. [22][19][23][46][49][50]

**A053** The weather sets the price of that line. The line decides who stays. Every time it was cut, the islands emptied. Attu after Alaska was sold. The lost villages after the war. The Japanese on Kiska in nineteen forty-three. Adak in the nineteen nineties. [22][35][39][46]

**A054** The one town that holds on is Dutch Harbor. It's America's biggest fishing port by volume, twenty-six years in a row. But across these islands, about four in five seafood-plant workers come from outside Alaska. [60][61]

**A055** And now the line is coming back. In April twenty twenty-six, the Navy and a federal agency set aside up to a hundred and fifteen million dollars for Adak's power, runway, port and fuel. [68]

**A056** In October twenty twenty-six, American fighter jets stood on alert on Shemya for the first time in decades. Chinese and Russian warships have sailed past these islands in recent years, in international waters. [69][70]

**A057** So the line is being rebuilt. Not for the people who left, but because someone needs these islands again.
