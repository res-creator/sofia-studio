# EP13 Antarctica had dinosaurs and palm trees. Why did it freeze?: sources

Researched 2026-11-21. Confidence: H = a primary source (paper, institution) states it, or several solid sources agree; M = figures vary by source, or the source is a press release / secondary; L = estimate / single secondary source. Say L items on screen as "about" / "reportedly", or drop them.
Wikipedia was used only as a pointer; no number below rests on Wikipedia alone.
Two PDFs were text-read directly: Pross et al. 2012 (*Nature*) and the ITGC results summary (Sept 2025). Most other papers were checked through their abstracts, journal pages and institutional press releases.

**Big correction for the script (new in 2026):** Antarctopelta is **no longer "the first dinosaur ever found in Antarctica"**. Barrett et al. 2026 (*Acta Palaeontologica Polonica*) show that a titanosaur tail bone collected by BAS geologist Mike Thomson on **9 December 1985** came out of the rock one month before Antarctopelta (January 1986). See #13.

**Title warning:** dinosaurs and palm trees did **not** live in Antarctica at the same time. Antarctic dinosaurs: ~190 Myr (Jurassic) and ~83-66 Myr (Late Cretaceous). Palms and baobab relatives: **~53-52 Myr (early Eocene)**, about 13 million years after the dinosaurs died. The title can stay ("had" both), but the script must not show a dinosaur under a palm tree in Antarctica.

## Human stories (for narration)

1. **David Elliot & William Hammer, Mt Kirkpatrick, 1990-91** (#8): two teams sharing helicopters; Elliot's geologists spot bones at ~4,000 m; Hammer's crew pulls 2,300 kg of rock in three weeks; the dinosaur is named for Elliot.
2. **Eduardo Olivero & Roberto Scasso, James Ross Island, January 1986** (#11): two Argentine geologists find armour plates in frozen mud; the animal takes 20 years to be named, and is named for Olivero.
3. **Mike Thomson, 9 December 1985** (#13): a BAS geologist bags a bone that sits in a drawer for 40 years until Mark Evans spots it; in 2026 it becomes "the first dinosaur ever found in Antarctica".
4. **IODP Expedition 318, Jan-Mar 2010** (#25): the *JOIDES Resolution* drills through 4 km of water off Wilkes Land, among icebergs; the mud brings up palm pollen.
5. **Scott, Wilson and Bowers, Feb-Nov 1912** (#5): Wilson collects Glossopteris at Mount Buckley on 8 Feb 1912; 35 lb of rocks stay on the sledge to the end; found with their bodies on 12 Nov 1912.
6. Backup: **Woodburne & Zinsmeister, Seymour Island, 1982** (#23): the first Antarctic land mammal, a marsupial jaw.

## Open questions (not resolved)
- Exact Cryolophosaurus age: Hanson Formation lacks a precise radiometric date (Rb-Sr 186 ± 8 Ma); "about 190 Myr" is a fair round number.
- Antarctopelta 4 m: no primary-source body-length estimate found.
- Kaikaifilu 10 m and Imperobator "Utahraptor-size": both are estimates from fragmentary material.
- The giant elasmosaurid (#20, ~11-12 m, ~15 t): figure from memory of O'Gorman et al. 2019; verify before using.
- Drake Passage opening date: estimates from ~41 to <30 Myr; Knahl et al. 2026 (AWI) not read (site blocked automated fetch).
- Gamburtsev peak heights: 2,400 vs 2,700 vs ~3,000 m above sea level depending on source; ice thickness over summits not confirmed from a primary source.
- Nothofagus beardmorensis age: Pliocene vs Miocene or older, still disputed.
- Bedmap3 thickest-ice value: ~4.76-4.8 km (press); exact figure not read from the paper.
- Glossopteris on the sledge: the NHM record ties the leaves to the polar party's Feb 1912 collection; one secondary source says the November 1912 recovery did not include Glossopteris. The polar-party collection is generally accepted as the source; phrase it as "fossils including Glossopteris".
- Trinisaura name honour (Trinidad Díaz): from secondary pointer.
