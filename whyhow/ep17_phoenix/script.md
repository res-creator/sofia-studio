## COLD OPEN

**A001** Okay, picture this. July twenty twenty-three, Phoenix, Arizona. It's a hundred and ten degrees outside. And it's been a hundred and ten or hotter every single day for a month. Thirty-one days in a row! [1]

**A002** And yet, right now, someone in Phoenix is filling a swimming pool.

**A003** Sprinklers are ticking on a golf course. There's a lake in the middle of the desert where people paddle around in kayaks. And about five million people are turning on their taps, and water just comes out. [2][3]

**A004** Here's the thing. Phoenix gets about seven inches of rain a year. That's less than a third of what falls on Los Angeles. This is the Sonoran Desert. Cactus country. [4]

**A005** So where does the water come from? Your first guess might be: they pump it from deep underground. Nope. For the city of Phoenix, groundwater is only about two percent of the supply. [5]

**A006** Second guess: they're wasting it, and they're about to run dry. Well, here's the twist. The average person in Phoenix actually uses about thirty percent less water than they did back in nineteen ninety. [6]

**A007** So how does a city this big exist in a place this dry? The answer is a story that starts more than a thousand years ago, with people who had nothing but sticks and baskets.

**A007B** We'll meet the desert's first engineers, a president who came to open a giant dam, a river that travels three hundred and thirty-six miles uphill, and the new rules that just changed everything. Let's go!

---

## CH1 — The first engineers

**A008** Long before Phoenix, this valley belonged to a people we now call the Hohokam.

**A009** Starting around fourteen hundred years ago, they did something amazing. With digging sticks, stone tools, and baskets of dirt, they built canals. Lots of canals. Archaeologists estimate hundreds of miles of main canals, maybe close to a thousand miles in total, built over many centuries. [7]

**A010** Some of those canals were wide enough to drive a car down today. And they worked with nothing but gravity, a tiny slope, mile after mile, carrying river water out to fields of corn, beans, squash and cotton. [7]

**A010B** And you can still see one. Right next to Phoenix's main airport, at the Pueblo Grande ruins, there's a park where an ancient canal bank sits a few minutes from the runways. Jets taking off over thousand-year-old plumbing. [7]

**A011** Then, around the fourteen hundreds, the Hohokam world changed. Their big villages emptied out, and nobody knows exactly why. Floods, droughts, conflict? Probably a mix. But their canals stayed, slowly filling with sand. [8]

**A012** Fast forward to eighteen sixty-seven. A former Confederate soldier and prospector named Jack Swilling rides through the valley and notices something strange: long, straight ditches running across the desert. [9]

**A013** He realizes what they are. Old canals! So he forms a company, his crew clears the old routes and digs new ones beside them, and soon there are farms again. A little town grows up around them. [9]

**A014** A well-read friend of his, Darrell Duppa, suggests a name. A new town, rising from the ruins of an old one, like the mythical bird that rises from its own ashes. Phoenix. [10]

**A015** And here's my favorite part. Today, some of the big canals in Phoenix still follow the routes the Hohokam laid out centuries ago. The city's plumbing is, in part, ancient. [7]

---

## CH2 — The dam with a president

**A016** There was just one problem. The Salt River, the river that fed all those canals, was completely unpredictable. [11]

**A017** Some years it barely trickled. Other years, it flooded and ripped the canal headgates right out. Farmers would go from drought to disaster in a single season. [11]

**A018** The fix? Store the water. In nineteen oh two, Congress passed the Reclamation Act, a law to build big dams for the dry West. And local farmers jumped at it. They put up their own land as collateral and formed what became the Salt River Project. [11][12]

**A019** Up in the mountains, about seventy-five miles from town, workers stacked giant blocks of stone into a canyon. On March eighteenth, nineteen eleven, a special guest showed up to dedicate it: Theodore Roosevelt himself. [12]

**A020** The dam was named after him. Roosevelt Dam turned an unpredictable river into a savings account. Fill it up in wet years, spend it in dry ones. [12]

**A021** Today, the Salt River Project runs about one hundred and thirty-one miles of main canals and around a thousand miles of smaller ones across the valley. That's more canal than Venice, by a lot. [13]

**A022** And here's a fun side effect. So much of the river now goes into canals that the Salt River through Phoenix is usually a dry bed. So when the city of Tempe wanted a lake, in nineteen ninety-nine, they built dams across the dry riverbed and filled it with water from somewhere else entirely. [14]

**A023** Which brings us to the most important "somewhere else" in Arizona.

---

## CH3 — A river 336 miles away

**A024** The Salt and Verde rivers still supply a big chunk of Phoenix's water. About fifty-eight percent. But the rest, about forty percent, comes from a river that doesn't even touch the city. The Colorado. [5]

**A025** The Colorado River runs along Arizona's western border, way out by Lake Havasu. For decades, Arizona had a legal right to a big share of it, but no way to actually get it to Phoenix. [15]

**A026** One man spent most of his life on that problem. Carl Hayden, Arizona's senator for more than forty years. In nineteen sixty-eight, near the very end of his career, Congress finally approved the Central Arizona Project. [15]

**A027** Construction started in nineteen seventy-three. Twenty years later, it was done: a concrete river three hundred and thirty-six miles long, from Lake Havasu, through Phoenix, all the way to Tucson. That's longer than the drive from Los Angeles to Las Vegas. [15]

**A028** But here's the wild part. Water doesn't flow uphill. And Phoenix is higher than Lake Havasu, Tucson even higher. So along the way, giant pumping plants lift the water up again and again. In total, close to three thousand feet. That's like lifting it over two Empire State Buildings stacked on top of each other. [16]

**A028B** And it's not all open canal. In places, the water rushes through tunnels drilled straight through desert mountains, and under washes, through giant siphons. A whole river, hidden in plain sight. [15]

**A029** All that lifting takes a lot of electricity. The Central Arizona Project is one of the biggest power users in the whole state. Basically, Phoenix doesn't just drink water. It drinks electricity, too. [16]

**A030** So now Phoenix had two rivers. Problem solved, right? Well, not quite.

---

## CH4 — The shrinking river

**A031** The Colorado River feeds about forty million people across seven states and Mexico. And for more than twenty years, it's been running low. Lake Mead, the giant reservoir behind Hoover Dam, dropped so far that old boats started showing up on dry land. [17]

**A031B** In the summer of twenty twenty-two, Lake Mead hit its lowest level since it was first being filled, back in the nineteen thirties. That white ring around its cliffs shows where the water used to be. [17]

**A032** In twenty twenty-three, the federal government declared a deeper shortage. Arizona had to leave five hundred and ninety-two thousand acre-feet of its Colorado River water in the lake. That's about a fifth of the state's share. [18]

**A033** An acre-foot, by the way, is enough water to cover a football field one foot deep. Roughly what two or three families use in a year. So we're talking about a lot of families. [19]

**A034** And Arizona is first in line for cuts. Its canal was the last one built, so its rights are junior. In August twenty twenty-six, a new federal plan set Arizona's cut at seven hundred and sixty thousand acre-feet a year for twenty twenty-seven and twenty twenty-eight. [20]

**A035** Then there's the water under your feet. In June twenty twenty-three, Arizona's governor, Katie Hobbs, shared the results of a new groundwater study for the Phoenix area. Over the next hundred years, demand would outrun supply by about four point eight million acre-feet. [21]

**A036** So the state stopped approving new subdivisions that planned to rely on groundwater alone. Not existing homes, not cities with their own surface water. Just new ones banking on the aquifer. [21]

**A036B** And this is a rule Phoenix wrote for itself. Since the nineteen nineties, builders in the Phoenix area have had to prove they have enough water for one hundred years before they can sell a single new home. [26]

**A037** That sounds scary. But here's the thing about Phoenix. It has been planning for exactly this moment for a long time. And that's where it gets clever.

---

## CH5 — The desert's clever tricks

**A038** Trick number one: never waste a drop, not even the gross ones. About fifty miles west of Phoenix sits Palo Verde, one of the biggest nuclear power plants in the United States. And it's cooled with treated wastewater from Phoenix-area cities. Yes, sewage. [22]

**A039** It's believed to be the only nuclear plant in the world that isn't next to a lake, a river or an ocean. Its water arrives through a pipeline from a treatment plant. [22]

**A040** Trick number two: save it for later. When there's extra river water, cities pour it into the ground and store it in the aquifer, like a giant underground piggy bank. The city of Phoenix alone has stored more than a hundred and eighty thousand acre-feet. [23]

**A041** Trick number three: swap farms for houses. In Arizona, farms use about seventy percent of all the water. An acre of crops can drink several feet of water a year. The same acre covered in homes often uses a lot less. [24]

**A041B** And people at home changed too. Green lawns gave way to gravel and cactus yards. Old toilets that used three or four gallons a flush were swapped for ones that use about one and a quarter. That's a big part of why each person uses so much less. [6]

**A042** And trick number four is my favorite. In twenty twenty-four, the Gila River Indian Community, just south of Phoenix, switched on solar panels built right over a canal. The first project like this in the United States. [25]

**A043** The panels make clean power, and the shade keeps the water from evaporating. Electricity on top, water underneath. The Hohokam would probably approve. [25]

## ENDING

**A044** So, how does Phoenix exist in the desert? Not with one magic pipe. It's ancient canals, a president's dam, a river pumped uphill across the desert, and a lot of very careful counting. [7][12][16]

**A045** It's a city that has to plan a hundred years ahead, because the desert never forgets to send the bill.

**A046** Remember that swimming pool from the beginning? Some of the water in it may have traveled almost two hundred miles from the Colorado River, climbed up and across the desert, and flowed down a canal the Hohokam might recognize.
