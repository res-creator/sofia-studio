# EP04 — How 7.5 Million People Fit Into Hong Kong

Format: How It's Run, English, voice am_puck speed 1.08 (~190 wpm), target ~11 min. ~70% real Hong Kong footage, cut every 2–4 s; grid graphics as short inserts.
Numbers in [brackets] point to SOURCES.md.

---

## COLD OPEN

**H001** This is one of the most crowded cities on Earth. Seven and a half million people, packed into towers so tall and so close together that some streets never see the sun. [1]

**H002** But fly a few minutes out of the center, and you'll see something strange. Mountains. Jungle. Empty beaches. Most of Hong Kong isn't city at all.

**H003** So how do seven and a half million people fit into a place that's mostly mountains? And why does a city with this much empty land have some of the smallest homes in the world?

---

## CH1 — A city with nowhere to go

**H004** Start with the map. Hong Kong is about eleven hundred square kilometers of land: a peninsula and more than two hundred islands. And most of it is steep, rocky hills that drop straight into the sea. [2]

**H005** Only about a quarter of that land is built on. The rest is mountains, forests, reservoirs and wetland. [2]

**H006** And about forty percent of Hong Kong is protected country parks, where you're not allowed to build at all. From the busiest street in the city, you can be on a quiet mountain trail in less than an hour. [3]

**H007** So almost all of those seven and a half million people live on a narrow strip of flat land along the coast and around the harbor. When you can't build out, there's only one direction left.

**H007B** It wasn't always this crowded. After the Second World War, and especially after nineteen forty-nine, hundreds of thousands of refugees poured in from mainland China. With nowhere else to go, many of them built wooden shacks on the hillsides. [20]

---

## CH2 — The vertical city

**H008** So the city went up.

**H009** Hong Kong has more skyscrapers than any other city on Earth. In early twenty twenty-five, it had six hundred and sixty-nine buildings at least a hundred and fifty meters tall. More than New York. More than Dubai. [4]

**H010** And most of them aren't offices. They're homes. Rows of residential towers, forty, fifty, sixty floors high, standing so close together that neighbors can see into each other's kitchens.

**H011** The most famous is in Quarry Bay. Locals call it the Monster Building: five old blocks joined into one giant wall of tiny apartments, air conditioners and laundry. It was finished in the early nineteen seventies, and thousands of people still live there. [5]

**H012** For decades, many of these towers were built and repaired using bamboo. Workers tie bamboo poles together with plastic strips, climbing dozens of floors with no metal at all. It's light, cheap and fast, and for a long time it covered most building sites in the city. [6]

**H013** But in November twenty twenty-five, a fire at Wang Fuk Court in Tai Po spread across seven towers wrapped in scaffolding and green netting during renovation. A hundred and sixty-eight people died. An inquiry later found that flammable netting and foam boards helped the fire spread. [7]

**H014** Even before that tragedy, the government had started moving public building projects from bamboo to metal scaffolding. One of the city's most famous sights is slowly disappearing. [6]

**H014B** And every one of those towers has to survive typhoons. When the Observatory raises signal number eight, offices and schools close, and the whole city waits indoors until the storm passes. [25]

---

## CH3 — Making new land

**H015** But building up isn't the only trick. When Hong Kong ran out of flat ground, it simply made more. [8]

**H016** For more than a century, the city has been filling in the sea with rock and sand to make new land. It's called reclamation. [8]

**H017** Today, only about seven percent of Hong Kong's land was made this way. But that seven percent holds some of the most valuable places in the city, including a huge share of its business districts. The famous waterfront skyline? Much of it stands where ships used to sail. [8][9]

**H017B** So much of Victoria Harbour has been filled in that in nineteen ninety-seven, the city passed a law to protect what's left of it. Today, the harbour can only be reclaimed if there's an overriding public need. [21]

**H018** The biggest project of all was the airport. The old airport, Kai Tak, sat right in the middle of the city. Planes came in so low over the apartment blocks that passengers could look into people's living rooms. [10]

**H019** Pilots flew straight at a hill painted with a red and white checkerboard, then made a sharp turn at the last moment to line up with the runway. It was one of the most famous and most nerve-racking landings in the world. [10]

**H020** In nineteen ninety-eight, Kai Tak closed. The new airport was built on an island that was flattened and extended into the sea, with more than nine square kilometers of brand-new land. [10]

**H020B** And the city still wants more. One plan, off Lantau Island, would create around a thousand hectares of new artificial islands. But it's so expensive that, for now, it has been put on hold. [22]

---

## CH4 — Life in a shoebox

**H021** So the city grows up, and it grows into the sea. And still, there isn't enough room.

**H022** For more than fifteen years in a row, Hong Kong has been ranked the least affordable housing market in the world. A typical home costs around fourteen years of a typical household's income, without spending a single dollar on anything else. [11]

**H023** That's why so many people live in subdivided flats. An ordinary apartment is split into several tiny units, each with its own family. Sometimes there's a bed, a kitchen and a toilet, all in a space the size of a parking spot. [12]

**H024** In the last census, more than two hundred thousand people lived in these subdivided flats. A typical unit was only about ten square meters. [12]

**H024B** Even outside those flats, space is tight. Across the whole city, the typical person has about sixteen square meters of living space. [24]

**H025** At the extreme end are so-called cage homes and coffin homes: bunk spaces barely big enough to lie down in, rented by the month. [12]

**H025B** Public housing in Hong Kong began with a disaster. On Christmas night, nineteen fifty-three, a fire swept through the hillside shacks at Shek Kip Mei and left more than fifty thousand people homeless. To rehouse them, the government built rows of simple concrete blocks. They became the city's first public housing estate. [20]

**H026** The government's answer is public housing. Today, about forty-five percent of Hong Kong's population lives in public rental or subsidized flats, in those huge estates of identical towers. [13]

**H027** But the wait for a public rental flat is long. In recent years, the average family has waited around five years for one. [14]

---

## CH5 — A city that moves like a machine

**H028** Now, when this many people live this close together, moving them around becomes a problem of its own. And this is where Hong Kong is surprisingly good.

**H029** The metro, the MTR, carries millions of journeys every day. And year after year, it reports that about ninety-nine point nine percent of passenger journeys arrive on time. [15]

**H029B** Paying is just as fast. Since nineteen ninety-seven, people have tapped a contactless card called Octopus on trains, buses, ferries, and even in shops. It was one of the first systems like it anywhere in the world. [23]

**H030** Then there are the old ways that never left. Double-decker trams have rattled along Hong Kong Island since nineteen oh four. The Star Ferry has been crossing Victoria Harbour since the eighteen eighties. [16]

**H031** Because the city climbs straight up a mountain, it even has escalators instead of streets. The Central to Mid-Levels escalator runs about eight hundred meters up the hillside and climbs about a hundred and thirty-five meters. It opened in nineteen ninety-three, and it's one of the longest outdoor covered escalator systems in the world. [17]

**H032** In the morning, it runs downhill, toward the offices. Later in the day, it switches direction, and carries people back up the mountain. [17]

---

## CH6 — The densest place on Earth

**H033** So how crowded can a place get? Hong Kong has held that record more than once.

**H034** The Mong Kok district in Kowloon was once named by Guinness World Records as the most densely populated place on Earth: about a hundred and thirty thousand people per square kilometer. [18]

**H035** But even that was nothing compared to what used to stand a few kilometers away: the Kowloon Walled City. [19]

**H036** It was a single block of buildings, packed onto an area smaller than four football fields. In nineteen eighty-seven, a government survey counted about thirty-three thousand people living inside. That's more than a million people per square kilometer. [19]

**H037** The buildings grew into each other until they became one solid mass, with narrow, dark passages running through it, lit by electric lamps, even in the middle of the day. [19]

**H037B** Inside, there were homes, factories, dentists, noodle makers and food stalls. And for decades, it was almost a place without rules, because neither Britain nor China fully governed it. [19]

**H038** Demolition began in nineteen ninety-three. Today, there's a quiet park where it once stood. [19]

## ENDING

**H039** So, how do seven and a half million people fit into Hong Kong?

**H040** They build up, higher than almost anywhere else on Earth. They make new land out of the sea. They live in some of the smallest homes in the world, and they move through the city on one of the most precise transport systems ever built.

**H041** And all of that so that, just outside the towers, almost half of the land can stay wild.

**H042** Hong Kong is a city with mountains on one side, the sea on the other, and nowhere to go but up.
