## COLD OPEN

**T001** Every couple of years, the schools in this valley do something unusual. Thousands of kids leave their classrooms and walk, some of them up to two miles, uphill. [40]

**T002** They're practicing for the day Mount Rainier sends a river of mud down the valley. In the town of Orting, after the alarm, they would have roughly forty minutes. [26][28]

**T003** And here's the strange part. The mud doesn't need an eruption. The last time a big one came, there wasn't one. [19]

**T004** So how do you live under one of the most dangerous volcanoes in America? And what killed the forest that's buried, standing up, under this town? [1][20]

## CH1 — A mountain of rotten rock

**T005** Mount Rainier is fourteen thousand four hundred and ten feet tall, the highest peak in the Cascades. And it carries more glacier ice than any other mountain in the lower forty-eight states. [1][2]

**T006** But the real danger is inside. For thousands of years, hot, acidic water from the volcano has been eating its rock, turning parts of it into soft, wet clay. Surveys from helicopters found the worst of it high on the west side. [6]

**T007** Put ice, water and rotten rock on a steep mountain, and one day a piece of it lets go. It mixes with water and becomes a lahar: a fast river of mud, boulders and trees, as thick as wet concrete. [11][31]

**T008** On the steep upper slopes, the biggest lahars can move faster than a hundred miles an hour. Down in the valleys, they slow to fifteen or twenty. Still faster than anyone can run. [31]

**T009** Lava and ash are not the main threat here. Ash usually blows east, away from Seattle and Tacoma. The killer that travels far is the mud. [51]

**T009B** And the eruption people talk about, in eighteen ninety-four? Seattle papers reported black smoke. A rival paper said it never happened, and no ash has ever been found. The last real eruption of lava was about a thousand years ago. [5]

## CH2 — Cities built on old mud

**T010** We know how far it can go, because it has already done it. About fifty-six hundred years ago, the top of Rainier collapsed. The wet, rotten rock became a gigantic river of mud: the Osceola Mudflow. [11]

**T011** It filled valleys more than three hundred feet deep and ran for over seventy-five miles. It even pushed back the sea. The valley where Kent and Auburn stand today was once an arm of Puget Sound. The mud helped fill it. [13][15]


**T013** And it wasn't a one-off. At least eleven big lahars have reached these lowlands in the last six thousand years. In two thousand two, the U.S. Geological Survey put the chance of another one in an average lifetime at at least one in seven. [16][17]

**T014** Around ninety thousand people now live in the mapped lahar zones, in towns like Puyallup, Sumner and Orting, and more than fifty thousand people work there. [14][23]

**T014B** Puyallup has the most people at risk. But Orting has it worst: most of its nine thousand people live inside the zone. [25][26]

## CH3 — The forest under Orting

**T015** In nineteen ninety-three, a geologist named Pat Pringle spotted something strange at a construction site in Orting: tree stumps, still standing upright, buried in the ground. [20]

**T016** Since then, more have turned up, whenever the town digs for new houses, a gas station or a school. Some are Douglas firs up to ten feet wide, buried right where they stood. [20]

**T017** They were killed by the Electron Mudflow, the largest lahar from Rainier in the last thousand years. Where it left the mountains, it was close to a hundred feet deep. By Orting, it still buried these trees up to fifteen feet down. [19][20]

**T018** For years, nobody knew exactly when it happened. Then scientists matched the growth rings of twenty-one buried trees. Four still had their bark, and all four died in the same year, in late summer. The year was fifteen-oh-seven. [18]

**T019** And here's what matters. As far as scientists can tell, there was no eruption. The Electron Mudflow started as a landslide on the west side, the side with the rotten rock, and nobody knows what set it off. The Puyallup Tribe has old stories of a flood of debris that buried this valley, likely this one. [19][6][21]

## CH4 — Forty minutes

**T021** In nineteen eighty-five, in Colombia, a small eruption melted ice on the Nevado del Ruiz volcano. About two hours later, lahars reached the town of Armero, built on old mudflow deposits, just like these towns. About twenty-three thousand of its people died. A hazard map showing Armero in danger had come out about a month before. But on the night, nobody could get the warning to the town in time. [47]

**T022** So in nineteen ninety-eight, the U.S. Geological Survey and Pierce County built a lahar detection system along these valleys. [33]

**T023** It started with tripwires and sensors along the rivers. Since twenty sixteen, it has been rebuilt with twenty-five new seismic stations, many of them listening for the low rumble of a flow, sending data in near real time. Their data reach emergency centers in about ten seconds, and two warning points are staffed day and night. [33][34]

**T024** When a big lahar is detected, sirens sound in the valley towns. For most of them, that means at least forty minutes, and up to three hours, of warning. Orting is near the short end. [28]

**T025** A new USGS map times the worst case. The mud reaches Orting in about an hour, Puyallup and Sumner in about three, and the port of Tacoma in about five. [27]

**T025B** That hour starts when the mountain lets go. Sensors need a few minutes to catch it, and the alarm takes time. That's why Orting is left with about forty. [28][34]

**T025C** Forget the thirty-foot wall of mud you may have heard about. In the model, the front reaching Orting is about thirteen feet deep, moving about nine miles an hour. Still enough to take a house. [29]

**T026** The rule is simple. Walk, don't drive, and get at least a hundred feet above the valley floor. Roads would jam, and the mud follows the valleys. [32]

**T027** That's why the kids practice. In twenty twenty-four, more than forty-five thousand students and staff took part in the largest lahar drill in the world. [40]

**T028** It's harder than it sounds. In a drill in two thousand six, the youngest children in Orting needed about twenty-five minutes just to reach the halfway point. [39]

**T029** So for about three decades, residents pushed for a footbridge over the highway that stands between them and the hills, so kids wouldn't have to wait at traffic lights. They call it the Bridge for Kids. Construction finally began in January twenty twenty-five, for about nine million dollars. A second bridge, over the river, still has no money. [41]

**T030** Even the sirens hold surprises. In May twenty twenty-three, an old, disconnected siren in downtown Puyallup went off by mistake. The county had to post a message: there is no lahar. [38]

**T030B** At noon on the first Monday of every month, the sirens play a chime, and a voice says: this is a test. The real alarm sounds nothing like it. [37]

## CH5 — The question nobody can answer

**T031** And there's one scenario the model doesn't answer.

**T032** In the U.S. Geological Survey's worst-case model, a giant landslide lahar reaches Alder Lake in about fifty minutes, and pushes out enough water to pour over the three-hundred-and-thirty-foot dam. What happens after that, the model doesn't say. [30]

**T032B** And a big lahar could move the rivers themselves. It's happened before. In nineteen-oh-six, a logjam sent the White River away from Seattle's side and south to Tacoma, and it stayed there. [49][50]

**T033** Meanwhile, the mountain is changing. Since eighteen ninety-six, its glaciers have lost about half their ice. One glacier was removed from the official list. [3]

**T034** As the ice melts, it leaves loose rock and sudden floods behind. One creek, Tahoma Creek, has had about thirty-five debris flows and outburst floods since nineteen sixty-seven. The road along it has been closed to cars since the nineteen eighties. [42]

**T035** And in July twenty twenty-five, Rainier had the biggest earthquake swarm ever recorded there: about thirteen hundred and fifty small quakes in about seven weeks. Scientists say it was hot fluids moving, not magma. The alert level never left normal. [45]
