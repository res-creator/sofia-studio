## COLD OPEN

**A001** Tuesday, November fourteenth, twenty seventeen. Nine forty-four in the morning, at Minami-Nagareyama station, just outside Tokyo. A commuter train pulls out. [10]

**A002** Twenty seconds early. [10]

**A003** Nobody misses it. Nobody complains. The next train is four minutes behind. But the company posts an official apology anyway: "We deeply apologize for the severe inconvenience imposed upon our customers." [10]

**A004** That story went around the world as proof that Japanese trains are perfect. But here's what the government's own data says. On Tokyo's most famous line, the Yamanote, the railway hands out official late notes on about fifteen of every twenty weekdays, mostly for delays of a few minutes. [13]

**A005** So it's not that the trains are never late. And it's not that the city is empty. At rush hour, some lines are still sixty percent over capacity. [17]

**A006** Every day, Greater Tokyo's railways handle around forty million trips. That's like ten New York City subway systems, running at once. [3]

**A007** So how does a metro area of almost thirty-seven million people move like this, without falling into chaos? The answer isn't one perfect machine. It's a pile of strange, very human tricks. [1]

**A007B** We'll start in the busiest station on Earth, then squeeze into a two-hundred-percent train, meet the railway that sells houses, and end with the day the ground shook under nineteen bullet trains.

## CH1 — The busiest station on Earth

**A008** Start at Shinjuku. Guinness World Records lists it as the busiest railway station in the world. [5]

**A009** In twenty twenty-two, about two point seven million people a day passed through it. That's four and a half times New York's Penn Station. Every day, a whole Chicago walks through one station. Before the pandemic, it was over three and a half million. [5][6]

**A010** It's not one station. It's several, run by five different companies, all stitched together underground. It's famous for getting even locals lost. [6]

**A011** And it was here, in nineteen fifty-five, the story goes, that Tokyo invented one of its most famous jobs. Station staff whose task was to push passengers into the trains, so the doors could close. [18]

**A012** The Japanese call them oshiya, the pushers. Many were students working part-time. At their peak in the nineteen seventies, Tokyo's rush-hour trains averaged two hundred and twenty percent of capacity. [18][16]

**A013** Some still do this job today, on a few lines, for about fifteen hundred yen an hour, around ten dollars. One young woman, who goes by Haruka, started in twenty twenty-one. She says it's mostly tucking in coats and bags, so the doors can shut. [19]

## CH2 — What two hundred percent feels like

**A014** Japan's transport ministry has an official guide to crowding, and it's surprisingly specific. [15]

**A015** At one hundred percent, everyone has a seat, a strap or a pole. At one fifty, you can open a newspaper. And at two hundred percent, there's, quote, "considerable body contact, but you can just manage to read a magazine." [15]

**A016** For years, the most crowded stretch in Japan was the Tozai subway line, between Kiba and Monzen-Nakacho. In twenty nineteen, it ran at a hundred and ninety-nine percent. [17]

**A017** Today it's down to around a hundred and sixty. But in one morning hour, sixty-four thousand eight hundred people still squeeze through that one stretch, on twenty-seven trains. That's about twenty-four hundred people in every train. A small town, every two minutes. [17]

**A018** Packing people that tightly created its own problem. Groping. Tokyo first tried women-only cars back in nineteen twelve, for schoolgirls. The modern version came in two thousand, and by two thousand five, most Tokyo lines had women-only cars at rush hour. [34]

**A019** And here's the most surprising part. Most small delays in Tokyo aren't caused by the trains at all. The ministry found that almost two out of three are caused by passengers. People holding doors, dropping things, getting sick, or just taking too long to get on. [14]

**A020** So when a train is late, the railway gives you a slip of paper. A delay certificate, to show your boss or your school. It's not a refund. It's an excuse note. And the government publishes how often each line hands them out. [12][13]

**A021** The worst line, the Saikyo, issues them on almost every weekday. The best, a small Tokyu line, about once a month. [13]

## CH3 — The railway that sells houses

**A022** So who runs all of this? Not one company. Dozens. JR East, Tokyo Metro, the city's Toei subway, and a crowd of private railways. And many of those private railways have a secret. They're also property companies. [4][23]

**A023** In nineteen twenty-three, a garden-city company and its new railway opened a line into the empty fields southwest of Tokyo. And next to the stations, they started selling homes, in a planned suburb called Den-en-chofu. [21]

**A024** The plots went on sale in August. Then, on September first, the Great Kanto Earthquake hit, and much of central Tokyo burned. But the new suburb out in the fields was barely damaged. And buyers rushed out of the city. [21]

**A025** The man running that railway was Goto Keita. He built the company that's known today as Tokyu. And he borrowed a trick from an Osaka railway called Hankyu. Build the line, own the land around it, sell the houses, and put a department store at the end of the line, so the trains stay full in both directions. [21][20]

**A026** It still works. In its latest financial year, Tokyu reportedly made more profit from real estate than from running trains. The railway's best product isn't the ticket. It's the home next to the station. [23]

**A027** And that's one reason Tokyo grew the way it did. Not one downtown surrounded by highways, but busy centers grown around the stations. [21]

## CH4 — The bullet train

**A028** Now, the most famous train in the world.

**A029** On October first, nineteen sixty-four, nine days before the Tokyo Olympics, the first Shinkansen line opened between Tokyo and Osaka. Its trains ran at two hundred and ten kilometers an hour. That's a hundred and thirty miles an hour, in nineteen sixty-four. [24]

**A030** But the two men who made it happen had both left. The railway's president, Sogo Shinji, and his chief engineer, Shima Hideo, resigned a year earlier, over the project's huge cost overruns. By one account, neither was even invited to the opening. [25]

**A031** Today, that line runs three hundred and eighty-three trains a day, for four hundred and sixty thousand passengers. And in twenty twenty-four, the average delay was about one point four minutes per train. Including typhoons and earthquakes. [8]

**A032** Then there's the turnaround. At Tokyo Station, JR East's bullet trains heading north often stop for only about twelve minutes. After the passengers get off, a cleaning crew has about seven minutes to clean the entire train. One person per car. Every seat turned, every tray wiped. [26]

**A033** People call it the seven-minute miracle. And it wasn't always like this. In two thousand five, a safety veteran named Teruo Yabe took over the cleaning company, when morale was low and complaints were high. [27]

**A034** He started treating the seven minutes like a performance, and let the cleaners suggest their own improvements. Harvard Business School later wrote a case study about it. [27]

## CH5 — When the ground shakes

**A035** Japan sits on top of some of the most active fault lines in the world. So the trains have to listen to the ground.

**A036** In the late eighties, a seismologist named Yutaka Nakamura designed a system that detects the first, weaker wave of an earthquake, and cuts power to the trains before the destructive shaking arrives. It went into use on the bullet train in nineteen ninety-two. [28]

**A037** On March eleventh, twenty eleven, a newer version of the same idea faced the biggest test imaginable. A magnitude nine earthquake. A seismometer on the coast caught the first wave, and power was cut along the line. [30][28]

**A038** Nineteen bullet trains were carrying passengers in the region. All of them stopped. None of them derailed. Nobody on board was hurt. [30]

**A038B** In fact, bullet trains have derailed only once with passengers on board, in two thousand four. Nobody was hurt then, either. [29]

**A039** But not every threat comes from the ground. On March twentieth, nineteen ninety-five, members of the Aum Shinrikyo cult released sarin nerve gas on five rush-hour subway trains in central Tokyo. [31]

**A040** At Kasumigaseki station, assistant stationmaster Kazumasa Takahashi picked up a leaking bag with his hands and carried it off the train. He died. In total, the attack killed fourteen people and injured more than six thousand. [32][31]

**A041** That's why, for years, you'd struggle to find a trash can in a Tokyo station. The bins were taken away after the attack. The few that came back are see-through. [32]

## CH6 — The next problem

**A042** So, is it perfect? No.

**A043** On January sixteenth, twenty twenty-six, a power failure after overnight work stopped the entire Yamanote line for about eight hours. Around six hundred and seventy-three thousand people were affected. The government stepped in and formally warned the railway. [38]

**A044** And Japan is running out of workers. The population is shrinking, and so is the number of people who want to drive trains at five in the morning. [35]

**A045** So in March twenty twenty-six, JR East raised fares for the first time since nineteen eighty-seven. And it plans to run the Yamanote line with no driver at all by twenty thirty-five. [35][36]

**A046** Crowding is creeping back up too. After falling during the pandemic, rush-hour trains in Tokyo have been getting fuller four years in a row. [16]

**A047** So that's how Tokyo moves forty million trips a day. Not with perfect trains. With pushers and late notes, railways that sell houses, crews that clean a bullet train in seven minutes, and machines that can hear an earthquake coming.

**A048** And with millions of people who all agree to the same rules. Line up at the marks. Let people off first. Don't hold the doors. That's why one company felt it had to apologize for twenty seconds. In Tokyo, every second belongs to everyone. [10]
