# EP18 — The Biggest City You Can't Drive To (Iquitos, Peru)

## COLD OPEN

**A001** Okay, picture this. A city of about half a million people. Traffic jams, universities, a Saturday night that goes until sunrise. Roughly as many people as Miami. [1][2]

**A002** Now try to drive there. From anywhere. You can't. [1]

**A003** No highway goes in, and no highway goes out. Every car you see on its streets, every fridge, every bag of cement, arrived on a boat. Or squeezed into the belly of a plane. [1][3]

**A004** Welcome to Iquitos, in the Peruvian Amazon. Guinness calls it the largest city in the world with no road connection. [1]

**A005** So it's a tiny jungle outpost, right? Nope. It's a real, busy city, bigger than Pittsburgh, and bigger than St. Louis. [2][14]

**A006** Then it must be on an island? Also no. Iquitos sits on the mainland of South America. It's just that the mainland around it is the Amazon rainforest, and three rivers wrap around the city like a moat. [1][4]

**A007** So how did a big city end up here? And how does it actually work, when everything has to float in? We'll start with the rubber rush that built it, then follow the river that feeds it, visit the neighborhood that floats, and end with the road that still goes nowhere. Let's go!

---

## CH1 — The rubber rush

**A007C** But first, a quick question. How do you even start a city in the middle of the jungle? You need a reason. A really, really good one.

**A008** Back in nineteen oh three, Iquitos was a river town of about nine and a half thousand people. Then the world went crazy for one thing: rubber. [5]

**A009** Bicycles needed tires. Then cars needed tires. And the best rubber came from trees that grew wild in the Amazon. So money poured up the river, and Iquitos boomed. [6]

**A010** Rubber barons built mansions covered in tiles shipped all the way from Europe. And on the main square there's a strange little building made entirely of metal plates: the Iron House. [7]

**A011** Tour guides love to tell you that Gustave Eiffel, the Eiffel Tower guy, designed it. Here's the fun part: there's no real evidence he ever did. It was bought abroad and assembled here, piece by piece, around eighteen ninety. [7]

**A012** That piece-by-piece idea was everywhere. One rubber baron, Carlos Fitzcarrald, needed to get a steamboat from one river to another. So his crew took the boat apart, carried it over a strip of land in pieces, and rebuilt it on the other side. [8]

**A013** A century later, director Werner Herzog turned that story into a movie, Fitzcarraldo, and filmed part of it right here in Iquitos. Except his crew dragged a whole ship over a hill. No special effects. [8]

**A014** But the boom had a very dark side, and it's worth saying plainly. The rubber was often collected by Indigenous people who were forced to work. [9]

**A015** In the Putumayo region, a company run by Iquitos rubber baron Julio César Arana was exposed in nineteen twelve by an American engineer, Walter Hardenburg, and a British diplomat, Roger Casement. Historians estimate tens of thousands of people died. [9]

**A016** And the boom ended almost as fast as it started. Back in eighteen seventy-six, an Englishman named Henry Wickham had carried about seventy thousand rubber seeds out of Brazil. [10]

**A017** Those seeds grew into huge plantations in Asia, which made rubber cheaper and faster. By the nineteen twenties, the Amazon's rubber barely mattered anymore. The money left. But the city stayed. [10]

## CH2 — Everything arrives by water

**A017B** So now you have a city of former rubber millionaires, deep in the rainforest, with no road. Which brings us to the most practical question of all.

**A018** So here's the big question. If you can't drive to Iquitos, how does a city this size get its stuff? [1]

**A019** Answer number one: the river. And not just any river. Iquitos sits on the Amazon itself, about two thousand three hundred miles from the Atlantic Ocean. That's roughly the distance from New York to Phoenix. [4]

**A020** And yet, ocean-going ships can sail all the way up here. A seaport, in the middle of a rainforest! [4][11]

**A020B** And you won't be alone on the water. The rivers around Iquitos are home to pink river dolphins. Yes, actually pink. Locals have all kinds of legends about them, and visitors take boat trips just to watch them pop up next to the city. [22]

**A021** Most supplies, though, come from the other direction. The closest town that's connected to Peru's road network is Yurimaguas. Trucks drive there, and everything gets loaded onto barges. [3][12]

**A022** From there, it's about three to four days down the rivers to Iquitos. Cars, motorcycles, fuel, beer, building materials, all floating along at walking speed through the jungle. [3][12]

**A023** People travel the same way. Big riverboats carry passengers who hang their own hammocks on the deck and swing their way to the city for days. Honestly? Sounds kind of amazing. [12]

**A024** Or you can just fly. Lima is about six hundred miles away, less than two hours by plane. For many people that's the only quick way in or out. [13]

**A024B** And flying here is actually an old idea. Back in the late nineteen twenties, Peru started an air service to Iquitos, with seaplanes landing right on the river. [24]

**A025** And there is one road. It's paved, it's about sixty miles long, and it goes to a small town called Nauta. And then it just stops. Nauta isn't connected to anything else either. [3]

---

## CH3 — A city on its own

**A025B** A sixty-mile road to nowhere. Keep that in mind, because it comes back at the end. But first, what does being this cut off actually do to a city?

**A026** Being cut off changes everything, starting with the lights. Iquitos isn't connected to Peru's national power grid. [15]

**A027** Instead, the city runs on its own power plant, burning fuel oil. Fuel oil that, yes, comes in by barge. [15]

**A028** There's been a plan for years to build a power line through the jungle, about three hundred and sixty miles long. It was signed back in twenty fourteen, and it's been stuck ever since. [15]

**A029** And when the river gets low, things get tricky. In the big Amazon droughts of twenty twenty-three and twenty twenty-four, rivers dropped to record lows across the region, and fuel barges heading for Iquitos got stuck. [16]

**A029B** Iquitos even has its own small oil refinery, about nine miles from the city, right on the Amazon. But its fuel still travels by water too. In September twenty twenty-six, one fuel boat sat stuck for days, simply because the river was too low. [25]

**A030** Now look at the streets. Because cars are so hard to bring in, Iquitos runs on three wheels. [17]

**A031** The mototaxi. Basically a motorcycle with a little covered bench on the back. One student study estimated around thirty thousand of them in the city. [17]

**A032** They buzz everywhere, like a swarm of very loud, very friendly bees. A ride across town costs about as much as a cup of coffee. It's the sound of Iquitos. [17]

## CH4 — The neighborhood that floats

**A032B** But the real star of Iquitos isn't on the streets. It's the water. And the water here has a schedule.

**A033** Okay, now the river does something wild. Every year it rises, and every year it falls. A lot. [18]

**A034** At the main port gauge in Iquitos, the Amazon can swing by around twelve meters between the dry season and the flood season. That's about forty feet. A four-story building of water, coming and going. [18]

**A035** So how do you live next to that? Meet Belén, the neighborhood people call the Venice of the jungle. [19]

**A036** Some houses here stand on tall wooden stilts. Others simply float, sitting on big logs tied to posts, so the whole house rises and falls with the water. [19]

**A037** In the dry months, you walk. In the wet months, from roughly February to July, the streets turn into canals, and you go to school, or to the shop, by canoe. [19]

**A038** Right next to it is Belén Market, one of the busiest markets in the Amazon. Fish, fruit you've never heard of, jungle medicine. It's loud, colorful and absolutely packed. [19]

**A038B** Try the aguaje, a dark red palm fruit that people here eat by the bagful, or as ice cream. Locals will tell you it's the taste of Iquitos. [23]

**A039** Life here isn't easy. Floods, poverty, and not enough sewers or clean water. But people have adapted to the river in ways a city on dry land never had to. [19]

## CH5 — The road that goes nowhere

**A039B** And when Belén throws a party, it really throws a party. Music, face paint and clown noses, right next to the water. [19]

**A040** So why not just build a road? People have been asking that for decades. [20]

**A041** The latest big idea is a highway north from Iquitos toward a town called Saramiriza, around two hundred miles of road through the forest, to finally plug the city into the rest of the country. [20]

**A042** Supporters say it would make life cheaper. Fuel, food, cars, all of it. [20]

**A043** But scientists warn that the route crosses some of the Amazon's biggest peat swamps and protected forests. And Indigenous groups along the way say they were barely asked. [20][21]

**A044** There's a reason they worry. In the Amazon, a new road usually means new loggers, new farms and new fires spreading out from both sides of it. [21]

**A044B** It's already happened here. When a smaller road was pushed north from the Amazon river toward the town of El Estrecho, reporters found new settlements, illegal logging and conflicts along it. [21]

**A045** So in a strange way, the thing that makes Iquitos so hard to reach is also what protects the forest around it. [21]

## THE END

**A045B** So maybe the real question isn't why you can't drive to Iquitos. Maybe it's whether the forest would survive if you could.

**A046** So, how does the biggest city you can't drive to actually run? With barges and hammocks, with a power plant fed by the river, with thirty thousand mototaxis, and with houses that simply float when the water comes. [3][15][17][19]

**A047** And remember that car we talked about at the start? It took a truck, a barge and a few days on the Amazon to get here. In Iquitos, that's just normal.
