## COLD OPEN — the experiment (≈ 0:00–1:40)

*Black. Door creaks open. White breath bursts into frame. Timer appears: 00:00. Thermometer: −50°C.*

**Y001** Let's try an experiment. You open the door, and you step outside. It's minus fifty degrees Celsius. The air is perfectly still.

*Timer 00:01. Macro of frost forming on eyelashes (stock).*

**Y002** In the first second, your breath turns into a cloud of tiny ice crystals. Within seconds, frost starts growing on your eyelashes. Every breath stings, because your nose has to warm air that's nearly ninety degrees colder than your body. [4]

*Timer jumps 00:10 → 02:00. Chart: frostbite time vs temperature.*

**Y003** Now the clock is running. At this temperature, in even a light wind, bare skin on your face can freeze in two to five minutes. That's why nobody here goes out with an uncovered face. [4]

*Hard cut: hand throws a pot of boiling water into the air → it explodes into a white cloud (stock or own illustration).*

**Y004** And if you throw a cup of boiling water into the air, it never hits the ground as water. It vanishes into a cloud of snow. [5]

*Reveal: night aerial of a big city in fog with lit windows. Pin: YAKUTSK 62°N.*

**Y005** Now here's the strange part. Three hundred and fifty-five thousand people live in this cold, every single winter. They go to work. Most days, their kids go to school. And inside their apartments, it's plus twenty. [1][9]

*Comparison bars: London January +5°C, New York 0°C, Yakutsk −37°C.*

**Y006** This is Yakutsk, in eastern Siberia. An average January day here is minus thirty-seven. If you live in London, that's forty-two degrees colder than your January. It is the city with the coldest winters on Earth. [1][2]

**Y007** So how do you keep a whole city warm, and running, at minus fifty? It takes a lot of tricks. We've counted seven. And the most important one is the one you'd never guess. It's hiding under every building in the city.

*Title card: "WHY DON'T HOMES IN YAKUTSK FREEZE?" — countdown dial 7 appears.*

*Map: Yakutia region highlighted; diamond icon; population counter 269,601 (2010) → 355,443 (2021).*

**Y007B** But first, a fair question. Why would anyone live here at all? Yakutsk is the capital of Yakutia, a region bigger than Argentina, and one of the richest in the world under the ground. The diamond company that works here mines about ninety percent of Russia's diamonds, and Russia produces about a third of all the diamonds on Earth. [27][31]

**Y007C** And people keep coming. Between twenty ten and twenty twenty-one, the population of Yakutsk grew by almost a third. In the coldest big city on the planet, they're still building new apartment blocks. [1]

## 7 — Outside is the freezer (≈ 1:40–3:20)

*Countdown card "7". Stock: frozen fish stacked like logs, open-air market.*

**Y008** Let's start with the easiest trick. In Yakutsk, from November to March, you don't really need a freezer. The whole city is one. [2]

**Y009** At the open-air market, fish is sold frozen solid, stacked like firewood, with no refrigerators at all. The traders stand outside all day, wrapped in so many layers that one of them described it as dressing like a cabbage. [11]

**Y010** And the cold even shapes the food. A local favorite is stroganina: raw fish, frozen hard, then shaved into thin curls and eaten before it thaws. [11]

**Y010B** Just outside the city, there's an even bigger freezer. Old tunnels dug straight into the frozen ground have been turned into an attraction called the Permafrost Kingdom. It opened in two thousand five. The walls are frozen earth, the sculptures are ice, and it stays below zero all year, even in July. [30]

*MYTH OR TRUE? card: "Yakutsk is the coldest city on Earth — all year round."*

**Y011** Quick question. Is Yakutsk the coldest city on Earth, all year round? Not quite. Over a whole year, Norilsk, far to the northwest, is very slightly colder on average. But no city of this size has winters like Yakutsk. Here, December and January both average around minus thirty-seven. [2][3]

*MYTH OR TRUE? card: "Yakutsk hit −62.7°C in January 2023."*

**Y011B** Here's another one you may have seen in the news. In January twenty twenty-three, headlines said Yakutsk had hit minus sixty-two point seven. It didn't. That reading came from a weather station elsewhere in Yakutia. In Yakutsk itself, it was "only" about minus fifty. The city's own record is minus sixty-four point four, set back in eighteen ninety-one. [2][28]

**Y011C** And the famous minus seventy-one in the village of Oymyakon? That number is an old, unofficial estimate. The official record there is minus sixty-seven point seven, from nineteen thirty-three. Still, a long way below anything most of us will ever feel. [29]

**Y012** That leads to the first big problem. How do you heat a city that cold, for that long?

## 6 — A power plant that heats a whole city (≈ 3:20–5:20)

*Countdown "6". Night shots of power-plant chimneys and steam (stock), then our diagram: plant → hot-water pipes → apartment radiators.*

**Y013** In most of the world, every house has its own boiler. Yakutsk does it differently. Big power stations burn natural gas, and they don't just make electricity. They heat water, and pump it across the city to the radiators in every apartment block. It's called district heating. [6]

**Y014** The newest of them, opened in twenty seventeen, can send out enough heat for a large part of the city on its own. The gas comes by pipeline from fields hundreds of kilometers away. [6]

**Y014B** Think about what that means. Your apartment isn't heated by your own boiler. It's heated by water that was hot in a power station kilometers away, pushed through insulated pipes across a frozen city, all winter, without stopping. And if that hot water ever stopped moving in deep winter, the pipes themselves would be in danger of freezing. [6][18]

*Calendar ring animation: heating ON from early September to mid-May.*

**Y015** And the heating season is long. By law, the radiators come on after five days in a row averaging plus eight or colder. In Yakutsk, that usually happens in early September. They stay on until around the middle of May. That's more than eight months of heating, every year. [7]

**Y016** There's even a legal minimum. In regions this cold, a living room is supposed to stay at least at plus twenty degrees. Outside, minus fifty. Inside, plus twenty. Seventy degrees of difference, held back by a wall and a window. [8]

**Y017** But a system like this has one weak point: it's shared. In October twenty seventeen, a fire at one of the city's power stations cut electricity and water across Yakutsk, and the region declared an emergency. It was fixed within a day. And it was still autumn. Now imagine the same thing in January. [10]

## 5 — Never turn off your car (≈ 5:20–7:10)

*Countdown "5". Stock: cars idling in a frozen car park, exhaust clouds.*

**Y018** Walk past a car park in Yakutsk in January, and you'll notice something strange. Many of the cars are running, with nobody inside. Sometimes for hours. Sometimes all night. [12]

**Y019** It's not laziness. At minus fifty, engine oil thickens, batteries lose power, and a car that's switched off may simply refuse to start again. So people keep the engine running, park in heated garages, or plug in engine heaters. Even the fuel has to be special. Ordinary diesel turns to jelly in this cold, so the north uses arctic diesel, made to keep flowing far below zero. [12][13]

*Our diagram: exhaust vapor → ice crystals; label "−40°C AIR HOLDS ~200× LESS WATER VAPOR THAN A WARM ROOM".*

**Y020** Remember the boiling water that turned to snow? Thousands of running cars do the same thing. Their exhaust is full of warm water vapor. But air at minus forty can hold about two hundred times less water vapor than the air in a warm room. So the vapor freezes instantly into tiny ice crystals that hang over the streets. [5][14]

**Y021** This is ice fog. On the coldest days, it fills the streets, and visibility drops sharply. The city, quite literally, disappears into a cloud it makes itself. [14]

## 4 — When school is cancelled (≈ 7:10–8:20)

*Countdown "4". Thermometer animation sliding down, with grade groups dropping off: −45 → grades 1–5 home; −50 → everyone home.*

**Y022** So when does Yakutsk finally say: it's too cold? For schools, there's an actual rule. In recent winters, when it's around minus forty-five with no wind, the youngest children stay home. At minus fifty, every grade switches to learning from home. [9]

**Y023** Which means that at minus forty, minus forty-two, minus forty-four, school is open. Kids walk there in the dark, because in December the sun is up for only about five hours a day. [9][15]

**Y023B** And then, in June, the opposite. The sun is up for about twenty hours, and the nights never get properly dark. Yakutsk lives half the year in the dark and half the year in the light. [15]

*Comparison: London December daylight ~7 h 50 min vs Yakutsk ~5 h 15 min.*

## 3 — A city with no bridge (≈ 8:20–10:00)

*Countdown "3". Map: Yakutsk on the Lena; the other bank with the road and railway; dotted crossing line.*

**Y024** Here's a fact that surprises almost everyone. Yakutsk sits on the Lena, one of the biggest rivers in the world. And there is no bridge across it. Not here, and not anywhere in the whole region. [16]

**Y025** In summer, cars cross by ferry. In winter, the river freezes so hard that it becomes a road, and trucks drive straight across the ice. [16]

**Y026** But twice a year, neither works. In autumn, the ice is forming but too thin to drive on. In spring, it's breaking up. For weeks at a time, the main road link from the other bank is cut, and only hovercraft and helicopters cross. [16]

**Y026B** In twenty twenty-three, for example, the last ferries ran in late October. The next ones only started again at the end of May, after the ice had broken up. In between, the city lived on the ice road and on whatever had been delivered by river during the summer. [16]

**Y027** That's why a bridge is finally being built: a cable-stayed crossing about three kilometers long over the river, and more than fourteen kilometers with its approaches. Its piers are being sunk on piles more than forty meters long. The current target is the end of twenty twenty-eight. [17]

## 2 — Pipes above the ground (≈ 10:00–11:20)

*Countdown "2". Stock + our cross-section: insulated pipes on supports running along streets.*

**Y028** Look closely at the streets of a city like Yakutsk, and you'll see pipes that most cities hide. Thick, wrapped pipes carrying hot water, running along the streets above the ground. [18]

**Y029** In a normal city, you bury your pipes. Here, that creates a problem. A pipe full of hot water, buried in frozen ground, acts like a heater. It melts the ice around it, and the ground starts to sink. So the pipes go up, onto supports, away from the frozen earth. [18][19]

**Y030** And that's a clue to the last, and most important trick. Because in Yakutsk, the real danger isn't the cold air. It's what lies under your feet.

## 1 — Protect the ice (≈ 11:20–14:30)

*Countdown "1". Our cross-section: Yakutsk ground, permafrost reaching hundreds of meters down. Archive-style illustration of a shaft.*

**Y031** In eighteen twenty-eight, a merchant in Yakutsk named Fyodor Shergin wanted a well. He ran the local office of a Russian trading company, and he needed water. So he started digging. [20]

**Y032** He dug, and he dug. For nine years. By eighteen thirty-seven, his shaft was a hundred and sixteen meters deep, and he still hadn't found a single drop of liquid water. Every meter of it was frozen. He gave up. [20]

**Y033** A few years later, a scientist named Alexander von Middendorff measured the temperature inside Shergin's shaft. It was below zero all the way down. He estimated that the frozen ground under Yakutsk went down almost two hundred meters. Today we know it's hundreds of meters thick. [20][21]

*Our cross-section: building with heat arrows going down → ground thaws → building sinks. Then: building on piles over an open gap.*

**Y034** This is permafrost, and the whole city stands on it. Frozen, it's as hard as rock. Thawed, a lot of it turns into mud. So if you put a warm apartment block straight on top of it, the heat flows down, the ice melts, and the building sinks and cracks. [22]

**Y035** That's why the apartment blocks of Yakutsk stand on concrete piles, with an open gap underneath. The gap isn't there to keep the cold out of the building. It's the opposite. It keeps the building's heat away from the ground, so the ice underneath stays frozen. [22]

*MYTH OR TRUE? card: "Houses in Yakutsk are on stilts to keep the cold out." → MYTH.*

**Y036** So here's the twist. Everything we've seen so far, the power stations, the heating, the running cars, all of it fights the cold. But this last trick protects the cold. Yakutsk can only stay warm on the inside if the ground under it stays frozen.

*Chart: ground temperature at 10 m at Shergin's shaft: +3°C since 1934. Then InSAR-style map with sinking areas (our graphic).*

**Y037** And the ground is warming. At Shergin's shaft, scientists have been measuring the frozen ground for almost a century. Ten meters down, it's about three degrees warmer than it was in the nineteen thirties. [23]

**Y038** Over one decade, accidents to buildings caused by thawing ground in Yakutsk rose by sixty-one percent. And satellite measurements show parts of the city sinking by more than four centimeters a year. [24][25]

**Y039** The coldest winters on Earth are not the city's enemy. They're its foundation.

## ENDING — back outside (≈ 14:30–15:30)

*Callback to the opening: door, breath cloud, timer — but now 20:00 and a warm window behind.*

**Y040** So let's go back to our experiment. It's minus fifty. You've been outside long enough. You walk back to your building, past the running cars and the ice fog, and you open the door. Plus twenty. [8]

**Y041** Now you know what's holding that temperature up: a power station burning gas from hundreds of kilometers away, pipes lifted off the ground, and concrete piles standing in ground that was frozen long before the city existed. A city that survives the cold by keeping the ground beneath it colder still.

*Comparison: Yakutsk record low −64.4°C (1891) vs record high +38.4°C (2011) — 103°C range; London record +40.3°C.*

**Y042** And one last thing. In summer, Yakutsk can hit plus thirty-eight. That's almost as hot as London's hottest day ever. From its coldest day on record to its hottest, a hundred and three degrees. [2][26]
