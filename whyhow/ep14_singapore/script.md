# EP14 — How Singapore Made Itself Bigger

Format: How It's Run, English, voice Fish Audio "Ethan" (s2.1-pro-free), target ~10 min. Mostly real Singapore footage and archive photos, cut every 2–4 s; maps and diagrams as short inserts.
Numbers in [brackets] point to SOURCES.md.

---

## COLD OPEN

**A001** Monday, August ninth, nineteen sixty-five. Noon. In a radio and television studio, a thirty-one-year-old prime minister is answering reporters' questions. And then he stops. [35]

**A002** "For me, it is a moment of anguish," Lee Kuan Yew says. "Would you mind if we stop for a while." The recording is switched off, so he can pull himself together. [35]

**A003** That morning, his island had been pushed out of Malaysia. It was now a country of its own. No army. No natural resources. And barely any land. [34]

**A004** Minutes earlier, he had said the thing that scared him most. "Our water supply comes from Johore." The country's water came from the neighbor that had just let it go. [36]

**A005** Today, that island is one of the richest countries on Earth. And it's not even the same shape. Since that day, Singapore has grown by more than a quarter. It built its own land, out of the sea. [2][3]

**A006** So how does a country make itself bigger? Where does all that sand come from? Why did its neighbors stop selling it? And what is Singapore building now, to stop the sea from taking it all back?

---

## CH1 — An island with nowhere to go

**A007** Start with the map. In nineteen sixty-five, Singapore covered about five hundred and eighty square kilometers. That's about two hundred and twenty-five square miles, a little smaller than New York City. [1]

**A008** Inside that space, it had to fit everything a country needs. Homes, factories, a port, an airport, an army, and water.

**A009** And it was packed. Many people lived in crowded wooden villages called kampongs. In May nineteen sixty-one, a fire at Bukit Ho Swee burned more than two thousand of those houses in a single afternoon, and around sixteen thousand people lost their homes. [32]

**A010** The new Housing and Development Board rehoused them in concrete flats within days. It became the model for the whole country. Today, about three in four Singapore residents live in a government-built flat. [32][30]

**A011** But those flats come with a twist. You own your home on a ninety-nine-year lease. When the lease runs out, the flat goes back to the state. Even your home is borrowed land. [31]

**A012** With six million people today and nowhere to spread, Singapore had only one direction left. Outward, into the sea. [33]

---

## CH2 — Moving hills into the sea

**A013** At first, Singapore made land out of itself. From nineteen sixty-six, engineers cut down the hills of Bedok and Tampines and dumped the earth into the shallow sea along the East Coast. [7]

**A014** That one project took about thirty years and added more than fifteen square kilometers of new coast. Today there are highways, apartment towers and a long beach park on top of what used to be water. [7][6]

**A015** Then came the airport. In April nineteen seventy-five, Lee Kuan Yew approved a plan to build a brand-new airport at Changi, on the far east of the island. Most of it would have to be built on sea. [13]

**A016** Five giant dredgers dug up the seabed offshore and pumped forty-one million cubic meters of sand and mud through ten kilometers of pipe. [13]

**A017** One engineer on the team later said: who would believe we could create so much land in two and a half years? [43]

**A018** Changi Airport opened on July first, nineteen eighty-one. The first flight landed at ten past seven in the morning. Today it's one of the most famous airports in the world, and it's still growing into the sea. [20]

**A019** Downtown, the same thing happened. The famous skyline of Marina Bay, the hotel with the boat on top, the giant gardens with their steel trees, all of it stands on land that was reclaimed in the seventies. [18]

**A020** And then, Singapore ran out of hills. To keep growing, it needed sand. A lot of sand. From somewhere else.

---

## CH3 — The sand wars

**A021** Sand sounds like the one thing the world will never run out of. But the right kind of sand is surprisingly hard to find. Desert sand is too round. Builders want sand from rivers, beaches and the seabed. [8]

**A022** And Singapore bought more of it than anyone. A United Nations report in twenty nineteen found that over twenty years, it imported five hundred and seventeen million tonnes. By far the largest sand importer on the planet. [8]

**A023** It came from its neighbors. Malaysia, Indonesia, Cambodia, Vietnam. Whole islands in Indonesia were reportedly dug away and loaded onto barges. [10]

**A024** And the numbers didn't add up. A U.N. study found that Singapore reported importing about a hundred and twenty million tonnes more Indonesian sand than Indonesia said it had exported. [9]

**A025** One by one, the neighbors said stop. Malaysia banned sand exports to Singapore in nineteen ninety-seven. Indonesia banned sea sand in two thousand three, and tightened it in two thousand seven, when it was supplying about ninety percent of Singapore's sand. [10]

**A026** Prices shot up. Building sites slowed down. So Singapore did what it does with everything it can't produce. It built a secret stockpile. Somewhere on the island there are hills of sand, kept in reserve for emergencies. The exact locations aren't published. [12]

**A027** Vietnam and Cambodia followed with bans of their own. And in twenty nineteen, Malaysia stopped all sea sand exports. By then it had been supplying more than ninety-seven percent of Singapore's imports. [10]

**A028** The sand is starting to flow again. Indonesia lifted its ban in twenty twenty-three. But Singapore had already learned its lesson. It needed ways to make land with less sand. [11]

---

## CH4 — Building land without sand

**A029** The first trick was to join islands together. Off the southwest coast there were seven small islands. Singapore filled in the sea between them. [21]

**A030** Together, they had covered less than ten square kilometers. Today, Jurong Island is about thirty, and it's one of the biggest oil refining and chemical hubs in the world. [21]

**A031** The second trick was to go down. Under the seabed next to Jurong Island, there are giant caverns, a hundred and thirty meters deep, holding one point four seven million cubic meters of oil. [22]

**A032** Putting that oil underground freed up about sixty hectares on the surface, around eighty-four soccer fields. At the opening in twenty fourteen, the prime minister said Singapore's land problem was a little bit like peak oil. [22][46]

**A033** And the third trick came from the Netherlands. On Pulau Tekong, an island off the northeast coast, Singapore built a polder. [14]

**A034** Instead of filling the sea all the way up with sand, engineers built a ten-kilometer wall in the sea, up to six meters high, and pumped the water out from behind it. The new land sits about one point two meters below sea level. [14]

**A035** It took close to half as much sand. But it needs pumps to stay dry, forever. The Dutch project director put it simply: the essence of a polder is that you need pumps to take the water out, so the land stays dry. [14][44]

**A036** The Tekong polder was finished in September twenty twenty-five. It added about eight hundred hectares, and it's used by the army for training. By the end of twenty twenty-five, Singapore measured seven hundred and forty-four square kilometers. Twenty-eight percent bigger than in nineteen sixty-five. [14][2][3]

---

## CH5 — Every drop counts

**A037** But more land doesn't fix the problem that made Lee Kuan Yew cry. Water.

**A038** Under an agreement from nineteen sixty-two, Singapore can take up to two hundred and fifty million gallons of water a day from Malaysia's Johor River. It pays three Malaysian cents for every thousand gallons. [24]

**A039** That agreement runs out in twenty sixty-one. So for sixty years, Singapore has been building what it calls the Four National Taps. [24][25]

**A040** Tap one: rain. Rivers and drains all over the island feed into seventeen reservoirs. In twenty-oh-eight, Singapore even dammed the mouth of Marina Bay, and the sea in front of the skyline became a freshwater lake. [25][19]

**A041** Tap two: imported water from Johor. Tap three: NEWater, sewage cleaned so thoroughly that it's ultra-pure. Most of it goes to factories, but some is added back into the reservoirs. [25][27]

**A042** On National Day in two thousand two, about sixty thousand people at the parade raised bottles of recycled sewage water and drank a toast to the nation. [27]

**A043** And tap four: the sea itself. Singapore now has five desalination plants. Today the country uses about four hundred and forty million gallons of water a day. By twenty sixty-five, that could almost double. [25][28]

---

## CH6 — The sea comes back

**A044** And now there's a new problem. After sixty years of pushing the sea back, the sea is rising.

**A045** Scientists in Singapore project that sea levels could rise by up to one point one five meters by twenty-one hundred. With high tides and storms, the water could reach four to five meters above today's average. [39]

**A046** About thirty percent of Singapore is less than five meters above sea level. In twenty nineteen, the prime minister said protecting the coast could cost a hundred billion Singapore dollars over the next century. [39]

**A047** So the next project is the biggest idea yet. Off the East Coast, Singapore plans to build a chain of new land called Long Island. About eight hundred hectares, around twice the size of Marina Bay. [38]

**A048** It will be built high, as a wall against the sea. And the water trapped behind it will become a brand-new reservoir. Land, flood defense and drinking water, in one project. Preparation work is planned to start in twenty twenty-seven. [38]

**A049** In the far west, at Tuas, Singapore is also building one of the largest fully automated ports in the world, on reclaimed land, with driverless vehicles and remote-controlled cranes. [40]

**A050** So that's how Singapore became bigger. It moved its hills into the sea, bought half a billion tonnes of other countries' sand, joined islands, dug under the seabed, borrowed a trick from the Dutch, and turned its sewage into drinking water.

**A051** All because of what one man said on a Monday in nineteen sixty-five, minutes before he broke down. A country with no land and no water can't afford to leave anything to chance. Not even the shape of its own coastline. [36]
