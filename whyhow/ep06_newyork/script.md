# EP06 — What's Hidden Under New York City?

## COLD OPEN

**N001** Walk through Manhattan on a cold morning, and you'll see something strange. Steam, pouring out of the street. Rising from manholes, and from tall orange and white chimneys standing in the middle of the road.

**N002** Most people think it's coming from the subway, or the sewers. It isn't. It's coming from a hidden system, more than a hundred years old, that most New Yorkers never think about. [1]

**N003** Under the streets of New York there's a whole second city. Pipes full of steam. Tunnels carrying water from a hundred and twenty-five miles away. A subway that never stops, and pumps that never rest. [1][2][7]

**N004** So what's really under New York City? And what would happen if it stopped working?

---

## CH1 — A city heated by steam

**N005** Let's start with the steam.

**N006** In eighteen eighty-two, a company started burning coal in Lower Manhattan, boiling water, and sending the steam through pipes under the streets to heat nearby buildings. It began with just a few miles of pipe. [1]

**N007** Today, that system is run by the energy company Con Edison, and it's the largest steam system in the United States. More than a hundred miles of steam pipes lie under Manhattan. [1]

**N008** It heats and cools around seventeen hundred buildings: offices, hotels, hospitals, museums and apartment blocks, all the way from the southern tip of Manhattan up to Ninety-Sixth Street. [1]

**N009** The steam leaves the plants at more than four hundred degrees Fahrenheit. Buildings use it for heating, for hot water, and even to run their air conditioning in summer. [1][3]

**N010** So why does it come out of the street? Sometimes a pipe leaks. More often, rainwater or groundwater touches a hot steam pipe and instantly boils. The orange and white stacks are put over those spots to carry the steam up above the traffic, so drivers can still see. [3]

**N011** Most of the time, it's harmless. But on the eighteenth of July, two thousand seven, a steam pipe under Lexington Avenue, near Grand Central, exploded. [4]

**N012** A geyser of steam and mud shot higher than the nearby skyscrapers, and a crater opened in the street. A tow truck fell into the hole. One person died, and dozens were injured. The pipe had been laid in nineteen twenty-four. [4]

## CH2 — Water from 125 miles away

**N012B** But to understand why New York sends its water through tunnels a hundred miles long, you have to go back to a time when it didn't. [12]

**N012C** Two hundred years ago, New Yorkers drank from wells and from a pond in Lower Manhattan that had become filthy. In eighteen thirty-two, cholera swept through the city and killed around thirty-five hundred people. Dirty water helped it spread. [12]

**N012D** So the city did something enormous for its time. It built an aqueduct about forty miles long to bring clean water from the Croton River. In eighteen forty-two, the water arrived, flowing almost entirely by gravity, and the city threw a huge celebration. [12]

**N013** Now, the water. New York uses about a billion gallons of water every day. And almost none of it comes from anywhere near the city. [2]

**N014** It comes from nineteen reservoirs and lakes in the mountains to the north, some of them more than a hundred and twenty-five miles away. [2]

**N015** And here's the clever part. Those reservoirs sit higher than the city. So about ninety-five percent of New York's water travels all the way to your tap by gravity alone, through aqueducts and giant tunnels, without a single pump. [2]

**N016** The water is so clean at the source that New York is one of only a handful of big American cities allowed to skip filtration. It's disinfected, but not filtered. [2]

**N016B** Inside the city, the water spreads through about sixty-eight hundred miles of water mains. And the used water leaves through about seventy-five hundred miles of sewers. Put together, that's enough pipe to cross the United States more than four times. [13]

**N017** But gravity can only push water so high. Once it reaches the city, it can climb about six stories. Above that, buildings need help. [5]

**N018** That's why the skyline is full of these. Wooden water towers. The city requires buildings taller than six stories to pump water up into a tank on the roof, and then gravity sends it back down into every apartment. [5]

**N019** There are somewhere around ten thousand of them, and many are still made of wood, the way they were a century ago. Wood is cheap, it insulates well, and a new tank can be built in a single day. [5]

## CH3 — The tunnel that takes half a century

**N020** All that water flows into the city through just two old tunnels: one finished in nineteen seventeen, the other in nineteen thirty-six. And for decades, engineers have had a terrifying thought. What if one of them failed? [6]

**N021** Nobody could ever shut them down to inspect them, because millions of people depend on them every second. So in nineteen seventy, the city started building a third one: City Water Tunnel Number Three. [6]

**N022** It's being dug hundreds of feet under the city, through solid rock, by tunnel workers known as sandhogs. They blast and drill in the dark, far below the streets, and most New Yorkers have no idea they're there. [6]

**N023** It's one of the biggest construction projects in the city's history. Twenty-four people have died building it. The Manhattan section was finally switched on in twenty thirteen. [6]

**N024** And the whole tunnel still isn't finished. More than fifty years after work began, the last sections are expected in the twenty-thirties. [6]

## CH4 — The subway that never sleeps

**N025** Above the water tunnels, and below the streets, runs the most famous thing under New York: the subway.

**N026** It's one of the very few big subway systems in the world that runs twenty-four hours a day, every day of the year. It has four hundred and seventy-two stations. [7]

**N027** And it has a secret enemy: water. Parts of the subway lie below the water table. Even on a dry day, workers have to pump around thirteen million gallons of water out of the tunnels. If the pumps stopped, parts of the system would start to flood. [7]

**N028** In October twenty twelve, the sea came in. Hurricane Sandy pushed a wall of salt water into Lower Manhattan, and it poured into the subway. Seven tunnels under the East River flooded. At South Ferry, the station filled from the tracks to the ceiling. [8]

**N029** The head of the transit authority called it the worst disaster in the subway's history. Salt water ate into the signals and cables, and some of the repairs took years. [8]

**N030** The subway also hides some beautiful secrets. Under City Hall, there's a station that opened in nineteen-oh-four, with arched tile ceilings, skylights and brass chandeliers. [9]

**N031** It closed in nineteen forty-five, because its curved platform was too short for newer, longer trains. But it's still there. If you stay on the number six train after its last stop downtown, the train loops through the old station, and for a few seconds you can see it from the window. [9]

## CH5 — The rock under the skyline

**N032** And under all of it, the subway, the water, the steam, there's the rock.

**N033** Much of Manhattan sits on an extremely hard, ancient rock called Manhattan schist. In places like Central Park, you can still see it breaking through the grass. [10]

**N034** For decades, people said this is why Manhattan has two clusters of skyscrapers, in Midtown and Downtown: that's where the rock is close to the surface. [10]

**N035** But when economists mapped the bedrock, they found the story is more complicated. The rock between the two clusters isn't much deeper at all. The towers went where the money was: banks and Wall Street downtown, and offices around the great train stations in Midtown. Where the rock was deeper, engineers simply dug further down. [10]

**N036** Digging down has always been dangerous here. To build the Brooklyn Bridge in the eighteen seventies, workers dug under the river inside huge wooden boxes filled with compressed air. When they came back up too fast, many got a mysterious illness, with terrible pain in their joints. [11]

**N037** Today we call it the bends. More than a hundred workers fell ill, and some died. Even the chief engineer, Washington Roebling, was left partly paralyzed, and his wife, Emily, helped carry the project to the finish. [11]

## ENDING

**N038** So, what's hidden under New York City?

**N039** A hundred-year-old steam system, heating buildings through pipes under the streets. Water flowing by gravity from mountains a hundred and twenty-five miles away. A tunnel that has taken half a century to dig. And a subway that never sleeps, kept dry by pumps that never stop.

**N040** Every day, millions of people walk over all of it without a single thought. And that's exactly how a city like this is supposed to run.
