## COLD OPEN

**N001** Every time it rains in New Orleans, the water has nowhere to go. So the city pumps it out, uphill, over its walls and into a lake. [16][23]

**N002** And all that draining is a big reason the city keeps sinking. [11]

**N003** A hundred and thirty years ago, almost none of New Orleans was below sea level. Today, about half of it is. Some neighborhoods sit twelve feet below the sea. [1][3][4]

**N004** So why doesn't New Orleans just stop pumping? And why does it depend on one set of floodgates, far up the Mississippi?

---

## CH1 — How a city sank

**N005** The first city stood on the highest ground around: a natural ridge of river silt, today's French Quarter. Behind it lay swamp. [5]

**N006** Around nineteen hundred, the city started draining that swamp with powerful new pumps, and built whole neighborhoods on the dried-out land. [1][13]

**N007** But the swamp soil was full of peat, made of old roots and plants. Once the water was gone, air got in. The peat rotted, the soil packed down, and the ground dropped. [6]

**N008** In eighteen ninety-five, a city map put the old swamps about a foot below sea level. Those same spots are now five to seven feet below. Parts of eastern New Orleans are twelve to fourteen feet down. [2][3]

**N008B** Measured from a NASA research plane, some areas were sinking up to two inches a year. [10]

**N009** And that's the trap. The lower the city sinks, the harder it has to pump. And the harder it drains, the lower it sinks. [11]

---

## CH2 — The pumps from 1915

**N010** The machine that made it possible was invented right here. In nineteen fifteen, a young city engineer named Albert Baldwin Wood put in a giant screw pump, the most advanced drainage pump of its time. Even the Dutch sent engineers to study it. [13][15]

**N011** Pumps of his design still work today, some about a century old. The biggest are fourteen feet across. [14][17]

**N012** And here's the strange part. Fifty-one of the city's hundred and twenty drainage pumps run on twenty-five hertz power. [19]

**N013** That's an old standard the national grid left behind long ago. So the city makes this power itself, and one of its turbines was installed in nineteen fifteen. [19][20]

**N014** When the pumps fail, the city floods. In August twenty seventeen, eight to nine inches of rain fell in a few hours. No drainage system here could keep up with that. But some pumps were down. The water board first said they all worked. It wasn't true, and its director stepped down. [18][22]

---

## CH3 — The river that wants to leave

**N015** Pumps handle the rain. But the biggest threat to New Orleans is the river it was built for.

**N016** The Mississippi has changed course many times. And for the last century, it has been trying to do it again, into a shorter, steeper path: the Atchafalaya River. [24]

**N017** In eighteen fifty, the Atchafalaya took less than a tenth of the river's water. By nineteen fifty, it took thirty percent. Engineers warned the Mississippi could switch within decades. [24]

**N018** If it did, the river past New Orleans could turn salty. The city could lose its drinking water, much of its port, and the fresh water its chemical plants depend on. [27]

**N018B** In twenty twenty-three, we got a preview. Salt water crept up the river, and the Army Corps built an underwater wall to hold it back. [28]

**N019** So in nineteen sixty-three, the Army Corps of Engineers finished the Old River Control Structure, far upriver: a set of giant floodgates with one job. Keep seventy percent of the water in the Mississippi, and let only thirty percent go. [25]

**N020** In nineteen seventy-three, a huge flood almost broke it. Part of a wall collapsed, and the water dug deep holes on both sides of its foundation. It held, but only just. [26]

---

## CH4 — The towns they flooded on purpose

**N021** For the river's floods, the city once had a harsher option. It protected itself by flooding someone else.

**N022** In nineteen twenty-seven, during one of the worst floods in American history, engineers blew up the levee at Caernarvon, south of the city, with thirty-nine tons of dynamite. About ten thousand people had to leave their homes. [29]

**N023** They were promised full compensation. Thousands got nothing. And the blast wasn't even needed: within days, levees broke upriver on their own. [29][30]

**N024** Today, a spillway does that job. The Bonnet Carré Spillway sends flood water into Lake Pontchartrain before it reaches the city. In twenty nineteen, for the first time ever, it had to open twice in one year. [31][32]

**N025** And after Hurricane Katrina flooded much of the city in two thousand five, the Army Corps built a new wall around the bowl, with levees, floodwalls, gates and a giant surge barrier, for fourteen and a half billion dollars. [33][34]

**N026** But the new walls are sinking too, and the sea keeps rising. In twenty twenty-one, the Corps proposed a three-point-two-billion-dollar plan to raise about a hundred and fifteen miles of levees, just to keep the same protection. [36]

---

## CH5 — Why not stop pumping?

**N027** So why doesn't New Orleans just stop pumping?

**N028** There are really two kinds of pumping. One pulls groundwater out of wells. Where the city stopped that, in the northeast, the ground has even risen a little. The other kind pumps out the rain. And that one can never stop. [8]

**N029** But the rain still has to go somewhere. About half the city is already below sea level. Switch off the drainage pumps, and the bowl fills up. [4][23]

**N030** We know what that looks like. South of the city, a drained farm called Delta Farms lost its levee in nineteen sixty-one, and again in nineteen seventy-one. Today, it's open water. [41]
