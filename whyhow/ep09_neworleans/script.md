## COLD OPEN

**N001** Every time it rains in New Orleans, the water has nowhere to go. So the city pumps it out, uphill, over its walls and into a lake. [16][23]

**N002** And every time it pumps, the city sinks a little more. [11]

**N003** A hundred and thirty years ago, almost none of New Orleans was below sea level. Today, about half of it is. Some neighborhoods sit twelve feet below the sea. [1][3][4]

**N004** So why doesn't New Orleans just stop pumping? And why does its survival depend on one concrete structure, far up the Mississippi?

---

## CH1 — How a city sank

**N005** Three hundred years ago, the city was built on the highest ground around: a natural ridge of river silt along the Mississippi. That's today's French Quarter. [5]

**N006** Behind it lay swamp. Around nineteen hundred, the city started draining it with powerful new pumps, and built whole neighborhoods on the dried-out land. [1][13]

**N007** But that swamp soil was full of peat, made of old roots and plants. Once the water was gone, air got in. The peat rotted away, the soil packed down, and the ground dropped. [6]

**N008** In eighteen ninety-five, a city map put the old swamps at about a foot below sea level. Those same spots are now five to seven feet below. Parts of eastern New Orleans are twelve to fourteen feet down. [2][3]

**N009** And that's the trap. The lower the city sinks, the harder it has to pump. And the harder it pumps, the lower it sinks. [11]

---

## CH2 — The pump that built a city

**N010** The machine behind it was invented right here. In nineteen fifteen, a young city engineer named A. Baldwin Wood put in a giant screw pump, the most advanced drainage pump of its time. [13]

**N011** Officials from the Netherlands wanted his pumps. But Wood didn't want to leave Louisiana. A newspaper joked that he went fishing instead. So Dutch engineers came to New Orleans to see them. [15]

**N012** Pumps of his design still work today, some about a century old. The biggest are fourteen feet across, and each can move about eleven hundred cubic feet of water a second. [14][17]

**N013** And here's the strange part. Fifty-one of the city's hundred and twenty pumps run on a kind of electricity the rest of America stopped using long ago: twenty-five hertz power. The city makes it itself, and one of its turbines was installed in nineteen fifteen. [19][20]

**N014** When that old power fails, the pumps fail. In August twenty seventeen, a storm dumped up to nine inches of rain in a few hours. Pumps were down, streets flooded, and the head of the water board stepped down. [22]

---

## CH3 — The river that wants to leave

**N015** Pumps keep the rain out. But the biggest threat to New Orleans is the river it was built for.

**N016** The Mississippi has changed course many times. And for the last century, it has been trying to do it again, into a shorter, steeper path: the Atchafalaya River. [24]

**N017** In eighteen fifty, the Atchafalaya took less than a tenth of the river's water. By nineteen fifty, it took thirty percent. Engineers warned that the Mississippi could switch within decades. [24]

**N018** If it did, the river past New Orleans would turn into a salty arm of the sea. The city would lose its drinking water, much of its port, and the plants along its banks. [27]

**N019** So in nineteen sixty-three, the Army Corps of Engineers finished the Old River Control Structure, far upriver, with one job: keep seventy percent of the water in the Mississippi, and let only thirty percent go. [25]

**N020** In nineteen seventy-three, a huge flood almost broke it. Part of a wall collapsed, and the water dug deep holes on both sides of its foundation. It held, but only just. [26]

---

## CH4 — The towns they flooded on purpose

**N021** And when the river rises too high, New Orleans has been saved by flooding someone else.

**N022** In nineteen twenty-seven, during one of the worst floods in American history, engineers blew up the levee at Caernarvon, south of the city, with thirty-nine tons of dynamite. About ten thousand people had to leave their homes. [29]

**N023** They were promised full compensation. Some got an average of two hundred and seventy-four dollars. And the blast turned out to be unnecessary: levees had already broken upriver. [29][30]

**N024** Today, a spillway does that job. The Bonnet Carré Spillway sends flood water into Lake Pontchartrain before it reaches the city. In twenty nineteen, for the first time ever, it had to open twice in one year. [31][32]

---

## CH5 — The wall around the bowl

**N025** After Hurricane Katrina flooded most of the city in two thousand five, the Army Corps built a new wall around the bowl: a fourteen-and-a-half-billion-dollar system of levees, floodwalls and gates. [33]

**N026** Its centerpiece is a surge barrier almost two miles long, nicknamed the Great Wall of Louisiana. And on the West Bank stands what the Army Corps calls the largest drainage pump station in the world. [34][35]

**N027** But the new walls are sinking too. In twenty twenty-one, the Corps proposed a three-point-two-billion-dollar plan to raise about a hundred and fifteen miles of levees, just to keep the same protection. [36]

**N028** Outside the walls, the land itself is disappearing. Since the nineteen thirties, coastal Louisiana has lost about two thousand square miles, most of the size of Delaware. [37]

---

## CH6 — Why not stop pumping?

**N029** So why doesn't New Orleans just stop pumping?

**N030** In one way, it already has. In the northeast of the city, where pumping of groundwater stopped, the ground has even risen a little. [8]

**N031** But the rain still has to go somewhere. About half the city is already below sea level. Switch off the drainage pumps, and the bowl fills with water. [4][23]

**N032** And under the streets, leaking water pipes now act like hidden drains, pulling the groundwater down on their own. [9]
