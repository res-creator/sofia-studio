## COLD OPEN

**A001** Tuesday, April sixteenth, twenty twenty-four. At one of the busiest airports on Earth, passenger jets are taxiing through water like boats. [1][4]

**A002** Over two days, more than twelve hundred flights are cancelled. Highways turn into rivers. Cars float in the underpasses. [4]

**A003** This is Dubai, a city that normally gets about half as much rain in a whole year as Phoenix, Arizona. That Tuesday, it got about a year and a half of rain in a single day. [3]

**A004** And in Al Barsha, Ann Celestial woke up to find that her building had no water in the taps. In the middle of a flood, residents were scooping water out of the swimming pool. [46]

**A005** Because here's the strange thing. Almost every drop of water in this city is made. It comes out of the sea, through machines. And the one thing Dubai was never built to do was get rid of water.

**A006** So how does a city of four and a half million people stay alive in one of the hottest, driest places on the planet? Where does the water come from? What keeps the lights, and the air conditioning, on? And what happens when the desert does something no one planned for? [41]

**A007** The obvious answer is oil money. But today, oil is only about one percent of Dubai's economy. The second answer is underground water. But the old wells are almost gone. So the answer is something else. And to find it, you have to go back to a time when Dubai had almost nothing. [32][14][20]

## CH1 — A town that lived on pearls

**A008** In eighteen eighty-one, fewer than twelve thousand people lived here, in a small trading town on a muddy creek. [28]

**A009** What it did have was pearls. A British survey in the early nineteen hundreds counted three hundred and thirty-five pearling boats in Dubai, and almost every man in town went to sea for the summer season. Divers dropped to the seabed again and again, from sunrise to sunset. [28]


**A011** One of them was Mohammed bin Abdulla Al Suwaidi, remembered as one of the last pearl divers of the Emirates. He spent more than fifty years at sea, and died in nineteen ninety-eight, at ninety-six. His grandson later left a diplomat's career to bring pearls back, on farms. [47]

**A012** Then, in the nineteen twenties, Japan started selling cultured pearls, grown on farms. They looked almost the same, and they cost up to ten times less. Then came the crash of nineteen twenty-nine. [29]

**A013** Within a few years, the trade that fed the whole coast collapsed. A manager at Dubai's pearl museum later put it simply: people got even poorer. It was starvation. [29]

## CH2 — Water from the sea

**A015** Until about nineteen sixty-one, Dubai had no clean public water supply at all. People drank from shallow wells and pools, and diseases like typhoid and cholera were common. [12]

**A016** The first fix came from underground. In the early nineteen sixties, a water engineer named Eric Tulloch found fresh groundwater at Al Awir, in the desert outside town, and pipes were laid into the city. [12][48]

**A017** Around the same time, the ruler, Sheikh Rashid bin Saeed Al Maktoum, borrowed money to dredge the silted-up creek, so bigger ships could come in. Dubai bet everything on trade. [30][49]

**A018** In nineteen sixty-six, oil was finally found offshore, at a field called Fateh. The first barrels left in nineteen sixty-nine. But Dubai never had much oil. Its neighbor Abu Dhabi got most of it. [31]

**A019** So Sheikh Rashid spent the oil money fast, on things that would outlast it. And in nineteen seventy-nine, at a stretch of empty coast called Jebel Ali, two of them opened in the same year. [13][34]

**A020** In February, Queen Elizabeth the Second opened a giant new port there. And in June, the same site produced Dubai's first desalinated water. [34][13][35]

**A021** Desalination means taking the salt out of seawater. And today it's how this city drinks. Almost all of Dubai's tap water comes out of the sea. [14]

**A022** The Jebel Ali complex alone can make about five hundred and ninety million gallons of fresh water a day. Guinness World Records calls it the largest single-site desalination plant on Earth. [15]

**A023** That's more than half of what the whole of New York City uses in a day. From one site. [15]

**A024** Most of it is made by boiling. Seawater is heated with steam from the gas power plants next door, it evaporates, and the vapor turns back into fresh water. [24]

**A025** Which means Dubai's electricity and its drinking water come out of the same machines. If the gas turbines stop, so does most of the water. [24][14]

**A026** And the city is thirsty. In twenty twenty-five, Dubai used about a hundred and ninety-four billion gallons of water. On its busiest day, almost five hundred and eighty-five million. [16]

**A026B** That's almost everything Dubai's plants can make. On its hottest days, the city runs with almost no spare water. [14][16]

**A027** And there's no going back to wells. Across the Emirates, the old groundwater is running out. Much of it is fossil water, thousands of years old, and in farming areas the water table drops by up to five meters, sixteen feet, a year. Here, wells are now only an emergency backup. [20][14]

## CH3 — The war on heat

**A028** Water is only half the problem. The other half is heat.

**A029** In August twenty twenty-five, the Emirates hit fifty-one point eight degrees Celsius. That's a hundred and twenty-five Fahrenheit, hotter than Phoenix has ever been. [9]

**A030** But the real danger is the humidity. In July twenty twenty-four, the heat index in Dubai hit around a hundred and forty-four degrees Fahrenheit. The air coming off the Gulf is like a steam room. [10]

**A031** So every summer, the law steps in. From June fifteenth to September fifteenth, outdoor work under the sun is banned from twelve thirty to three in the afternoon. Companies that break it are fined for every worker. [11]

**A032** For the people who build and clean this city, that break is the difference between a hard day and a deadly one.

**A033** Everyone else lives indoors, and indoors means air conditioning. On a summer afternoon, cooling can take up as much as sixty percent of the city's peak electricity. [22]

**A034** In twenty twenty-five, Dubai's peak demand passed eleven gigawatts, and this summer it set a new record. That's the output of more than five Hoover Dams, and much of it goes into keeping air cold. [21]

**A035** So Dubai doesn't just cool buildings one by one. It pipes cold to them.

**A036** It's called district cooling. Giant central plants chill water and send it through underground pipes to whole neighborhoods. The biggest company doing this, Empower, says it's the largest district cooling provider in the world. [25]

**A037** In two thousand four it had a tiny network. Today it's connected to about seventeen hundred and fifty buildings, and one of its plants, in Business Bay, holds a Guinness record as the biggest of its kind. [25]

**A038** And the power for all of it? Most of it still comes from natural gas. South of the city, in the desert, there's a solar park with about three point nine gigawatts of capacity, nearly two Hoover Dams. But last year, clean energy still made only about a sixth of Dubai's power. [23][21]

## CH4 — A city that imports everything

**A039** Water from the sea. Cold from pipes. But there's one thing a desert can't make at all. Food.

**A040** The Emirates import about eighty-five to ninety percent of their food. Nearly nine of every ten bites arrive by ship or by plane. [26]

**A041** That's why Jebel Ali port matters so much. It's one of the busiest container ports in the world, and it's the city's lifeline. [34]

**A042** Dubai is trying to grow some of its own. Near the new airport, there's an indoor vertical farm called Bustanica, billed as the largest in the world. It grows about three thousand kilos of salad greens a day, around six thousand pounds, mostly for airline meals. It's a drop in the bucket, but it's a start. [27]

**A043** And here's a strange one. A city in the desert also imports sand.

**A044** Desert sand is the wrong kind. The wind has polished the grains round and smooth, so they don't lock together in concrete. Builders need rough, angular sand. [38]

**A045** Even Palm Jumeirah, the island shaped like a palm tree, wasn't made from the desert. It was built from about ninety-four million cubic meters of sand dredged from the bottom of the Gulf, enough to pour Hoover Dam almost forty times, and sprayed into place, guided by GPS. [37]

**A046** And on that imported, engineered ground, the city exploded. In nineteen sixty-eight, Dubai had fifty-nine thousand people. By the end of twenty twenty-five, it had four point five eight million. More than the city of Los Angeles. [39][41]

**A047** Oil started it. But today the city runs on trade, flights, tourism, finance and property. On being a place people want to come to. [32][33]

## CH5 — The day the desert flooded

**A048** Which brings us back to that Tuesday in April.

**A049** Dubai had built machines to make water, machines to make cold, and a port to bring in everything else. What it had never really built was a way to drain the rain. [6]

**A050** In a city where it almost never rains, there was no network big enough to carry a storm away. So the water sat in the roads, the underpasses and the basements. [6]

**A050B** Farheen fled her ground-floor flat with her one-year-old as the water came through the ceiling. "All our apartments were ruined," she said. [46]

**A051** Five people died in the Emirates. Insurers estimated the losses at around three billion dollars. [5]

**A052** And almost immediately, people online blamed cloud seeding. It's true that the Emirates fly planes that fire salt flares into clouds to squeeze out a bit more rain. [43]

**A053** But the weather service said it ran no seeding flights during that storm. And seeding can only nudge clouds that are already there, by maybe ten to fifteen percent. It can't make a storm like that. [8][43]

**A054** Scientists found signs that warming may have made rain like this ten to forty percent heavier, though the data were thin. A desert city now has to prepare for floods as well as heat. [7]

**A055** Two months later, Dubai approved a drainage project called Tasreef. Thirty billion dirhams, about eight billion dollars, to raise the city's rainwater drainage capacity by seven hundred percent by twenty thirty-three. [6]

**A056** At the same time, it's building one of the world's largest seawater plants that filter out salt with membranes instead of boiling it, at Hassyan. And by twenty thirty, the plan is to make all desalinated water with clean energy and waste heat. [17]

**A057** So that's how Dubai stays alive. It makes its water from the sea, pipes cold under its streets, ships in its food and even its sand, and spends whatever it takes to keep the desert outside.
