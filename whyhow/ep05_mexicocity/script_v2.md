## COLD OPEN

**X001** This is the Angel of Independence, in Mexico City. When it opened in nineteen ten, you climbed nine steps to reach it. Today, there are twenty-three. [12]

**X002** Nobody built it taller. The column stands on thousands of wooden piles, so it stayed where it was. The street around it sank. [12][13]

**X003** In the worst parts of this city, the ground drops more than a foot a year. And the height it loses never comes back. [1][41]

**X004** So how do you run a city of more than twenty million people on ground that keeps falling away? And why can't anyone stop it?

---

## CH1 — The lake under the streets

**X005** Here's the short version. Mexico City sits on the bottom of a drained lake. Under its streets lies a thick layer of soft clay, and that clay is about seventy percent water. [22]

**X006** For more than a century, the city has been drinking the water underneath it. About seventy percent of its supply is groundwater. [31]

**X007** Pump the water out, and the clay squeezes flat, like a wet sponge under a brick. Everything on top goes down with it. [26]

**X008** It has already gone down a long way. In nineteen ten, the city center stood almost two meters above the level of the old lake. By nineteen fifty-nine, it was five and a half meters below it. The city had sunk below its own lake. And that would turn its sewers upside down. [8]

---

## CH2 — The lake they had to kill

**X009** It didn't start that way. The Aztec capital, Tenochtitlan, stood on an island in the lake, with canals instead of streets. To protect it, they built a dike about sixteen kilometers long, holding back the salty part of the lake. [23]

**X010** The Spanish built their capital on its ruins, and they hated the floods. In sixteen-oh-seven, about forty thousand workers with hand tools started digging a drain through the mountains. [24]

**X011** It got blocked. In sixteen twenty-nine, the city flooded, and parts of it stayed under water for about five years. [24]

**X012** It took almost three centuries, but they won. In nineteen hundred, the Gran Canal opened, and the lake drained away. Then the city started drilling wells into the old lakebed. [25][26]

---

## CH3 — The cathedral that was straightened

**X013** When the ground sinks unevenly, buildings tilt.

**X014** The Metropolitan Cathedral was already sinking while it was being built. Its builders hid it, wedging in stones and making the cornices uneven, so it would look straight. [14]

**X015** By nineteen eighty-nine, one end of the cathedral was almost two and a half meters lower than the other. [14]

**X016** The rescue was one of the strangest engineering jobs ever done. Engineers dug thirty-two shafts beside it, and pulled clay out from under the high side through narrow pipes, a little at a time, about one and a half million times. Over five years, the cathedral came back by almost a meter. [15]

**X017** A few blocks away, the Palace of Fine Arts has sunk so far that its original entrance hall is now the basement. Visitors walk in through what used to be the first floor. [17]

**X018** And near the Monument to the Revolution, an old steel well pipe, anchored deep underground, now sticks more than seven meters out of the pavement. It isn't growing. The street is falling away from it. [18]

---

## CH4 — Sewage that has to go uphill

**X019** Remember the city sinking below its own lake? That broke the drains.

**X020** Sewage only flows downhill. But by the nineteen fifties, the city's sewers sat four meters below the canal they were supposed to empty into. In the floods of nineteen fifty and fifty-one, people crossed the city center in boats. [35]

**X021** So Mexico City started pumping its sewage uphill. And in nineteen sixty-seven, engineers made a radical decision: dig drains so deep that however far the city sank, they would never stop working. [35][36]

**X022** The first deep tunnel opened in nineteen seventy-five, with shafts more than two hundred meters deep. And in twenty nineteen, a second one opened, sixty-two kilometers long. It's the longest sewage tunnel in the world. [36][38]

---

## CH5 — Thirsty and flooded

**X023** And here's the paradox. A city built on a lake is running out of water.

**X024** More than a quarter of its water is pumped from reservoirs in the mountains, lifted more than eleven hundred meters uphill. In twenty twenty-four, after a long drought, those reservoirs fell to about a quarter full, and some homes got water for an hour a week. [31][32][33]

**X025** As the ground sinks, it bends and cracks the pipes. Around forty percent of the city's water is lost before it reaches a tap. [34]

**X026** Even the metro feels it. On Line 5, the sinking has bent the track into a slope almost twice as steep as the safe limit for braking. [39]

---

## CH6 — Can it be stopped?

**X027** So, can Mexico City stop sinking?

**X028** Not really. Scientists found that today's sinking barely depends on how much water is pumped today. The clay is still draining from decades of past pumping, and the lost height is gone for good. [28][41]

**X029** What the city can do is slow it down. It is fixing leaks, pushing clean water back into the ground, and since twenty nineteen, it has put more than seventy thousand rain-collecting systems on people's roofs. [42][43]

---

## ENDING

**X030** So, why is Mexico City sinking?

**X031** Because it drained the lake it was built on, and then drank the water out of the mud. The clay squeezed, the city fell below its own lake, and now even its sewage has to be pumped uphill.

**X032** And in the middle of it all, the Angel of Independence stands still, while the city drops around it, one step at a time.

**X033** So, would you live in a city that sinks a little every day? And which city should we explain next? Tell me in the comments. This was How It's Run.
