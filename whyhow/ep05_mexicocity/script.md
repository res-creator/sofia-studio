# EP05 — The City Sinking Faster Than Venice (Mexico City)

Format: How It's Run, English, voice am_puck speed 1.08 (~190 wpm), target ~10 min. Mostly real Mexico City footage/photos, cut every 2–4 s; grid graphics as short inserts.
Numbers in [brackets] point to SOURCES.md.

---

## COLD OPEN

**M001** This is the Angel of Independence, in the heart of Mexico City. When it was finished in nineteen ten, you climbed nine steps to reach its base. [1]

**M002** Today, there are fourteen more. Nobody made the monument taller. Its foundations go deep, down to firmer ground, so the column stayed where it was. The city around it sank. [1]

**M003** Mexico City is one of the fastest-sinking big cities on Earth. In some neighborhoods, the ground drops by up to half a meter every single year. [2]

**M004** Venice sinks by a few millimeters a year. This city, in places, more than a hundred times faster. So why is a city of more than twenty million people falling into the ground? [2][3]

---

## CH1 — A city built on a lake

**M005** The answer starts seven hundred years ago, with water.

**M006** In the thirteen twenties, the Mexica people, the Aztecs, founded their capital, Tenochtitlan, on a small island in the middle of a lake: Lake Texcoco, high in a valley surrounded by mountains and volcanoes. [4]

**M007** They connected the island to the shore with long causeways, built canals instead of streets, and grew food on floating gardens made of mud and reeds, called chinampas. By the time the Spanish arrived, it was one of the largest cities in the world. [4]

**M007B** When Spanish soldiers first saw it in fifteen nineteen, they couldn't believe their eyes. One of them, Bernal Díaz, later wrote that the towers and temples rising out of the water looked like something from a book of enchantments. Some of the soldiers, he said, asked whether it was all a dream. [11]

**M008** In fifteen twenty-one, the Spanish conquered Tenochtitlan and built their own capital right on top of its ruins. But they didn't like the lake. [4]

**M009** The valley has no natural outlet to the sea, so in the rainy season, the water had nowhere to go, and the new city flooded again and again. So, over the following centuries, they decided to get rid of the lake. They dug canals and tunnels through the mountains and drained it. [4][5]

**M009B** It took a very long time. In sixteen twenty-nine, a huge flood put the city under water, and parts of it stayed flooded for about five years. People moved around in canoes, and the engineer in charge of the drainage tunnel was thrown in jail. [5]

**M010** The lake disappeared. The city spread across the old lakebed. And that is the problem. Because what's left under Mexico City isn't rock. It's the bottom of a lake.

---

## CH2 — Why the ground sinks

**M011** Under much of the city lies a thick layer of soft clay, the old mud of the lake. And that clay is mostly water, like a sponge that's soaked through. [2]

**M012** Below the clay are aquifers, layers of rock and gravel full of groundwater. And for more than a century, Mexico City has been drinking from them. Today, around sixty percent of the city's water is pumped up from under its own streets. [6]

**M013** Pump the water out, and the clay above slowly gives up its water too. It shrinks, the way a sponge flattens when you squeeze it. And the city on top goes down with it. [2]

**M014** And here's the worst part. Once that clay is squeezed, it doesn't spring back. Even if the pumping stopped tomorrow, the ground would not come back up. The sinking is permanent. [2]

**M015** In the last century, parts of the city have dropped by more than twelve meters. That's the height of a four-storey building. [7]

**M016** In twenty twenty-six, a new radar satellite built by NASA and India's space agency mapped the city from orbit. It found areas sinking by more than two centimeters a month. [7]

**M016B** You can see the damage from space, but you can also see it from the street. Pavements tilt. Old buildings lean on their neighbors. And at the base of some monuments, the ground has dropped so far that new steps had to be added, over and over again. [1][7]

---

## CH3 — The buildings that lean

**M017** When the ground sinks evenly, you barely notice. The trouble starts when it sinks unevenly. One corner of a building drops faster than the other, and the whole thing starts to lean.

**M018** Look at the Metropolitan Cathedral on the main square, the Zócalo. Construction began in the fifteen hundreds, on top of the ruins of Aztec temples. For centuries, it has been sinking unevenly into the clay. [8]

**M018B** Right next to it lie the ruins of the Templo Mayor, the great Aztec temple. They were rediscovered in nineteen seventy-eight, when electricity workers digging under the street hit a giant carved stone. [14]

**M019** By the nineteen nineties, it was in serious trouble. One side of the foundation was more than one and a half meters lower than the other, and cracks were running through the building. [8]

**M020** So engineers did something surprising. Instead of pushing the low side up, they carefully dug soil out from under the high side, to let it sink too, and bring the cathedral back toward level. Then they injected grout into the ground to slow it down. [8]

**M021** A few blocks away is the Palace of Fine Arts, the Palacio de Bellas Artes, with its white marble walls and golden domes. Heavy marble, on soft clay. Since construction began in nineteen-oh-four, it has sunk by several meters. [9]

**M022** All over the city, you can see the same story. Leaning churches. Buildings tilting toward each other. Streets that crack and buckle. Doors and windows that no longer close.

**M022B** The soft lake clay has another dangerous side. When an earthquake hits, it shakes like jelly, and it can make the shaking much stronger than on solid rock. [12]

**M022C** On the nineteenth of September, nineteen eighty-five, a powerful earthquake struck the Pacific coast, more than three hundred kilometers away. On solid ground, it did limited damage. But in the old lakebed, the clay amplified the waves. Hundreds of buildings collapsed. The official death toll was around ten thousand, and many believe the real number was higher. [12]

---

## CH4 — Thirsty and flooded at the same time

**M023** And now the strangest part. A city built on a lake is running short of water.

**M024** The rest of the city's water comes from far away, through the Cutzamala system: reservoirs in the mountains, and water pumped uphill over a mountain range to reach the capital. [6]

**M025** In twenty twenty-four, after years of drought, those reservoirs fell to around a quarter of their capacity. Headlines warned of a "Day Zero", when the taps could run dry. [6]

**M026** It didn't happen. The rains came back. But for millions of people, water already arrives only a few days a week, or by truck. [6]

**M026B** Across the city, water trucks called pipas drive from street to street, filling tanks on rooftops. On some streets, people line up with buckets. [6]

**M027** And a lot of it never even reaches a tap. As the ground sinks, it bends and breaks the pipes beneath the streets. By some estimates, around forty percent of the city's water is lost to leaks. [10]

**M028** Then, in the rainy season, the opposite happens. Heavy storms hit, and the streets flood. For five hundred years, the city's water system was built to do one thing: get water out of the valley as fast as possible. [10]

**M029** So Mexico City floods in summer, runs dry in spring, and sinks all year round.

---

## CH5 — Can it be stopped?

**M030** So, can Mexico City stop sinking?

**M031** Scientists studying the clay say the squeezing that has already started will keep going for a long time. One study estimated it could continue for around a hundred and fifty years, and add up to thirty meters more in the worst places. [2]

**M032** Pumping less would slow it down. So the city is trying to find other water: collecting rain from rooftops, fixing leaking pipes, and pushing more water back into the ground. [6]

**M033** And there's one place where you can still see what this valley used to be. In Xochimilco, in the south of the city, the old canals survive, with colorful boats and floating gardens, the last pieces of the great lake. [4]

**M033B** Today, Xochimilco and the historic center of Mexico City are a UNESCO World Heritage Site. On weekends, families float along the canals, with music, food and flowers, on water the Aztecs once farmed. [13]

---

## ENDING

**M034** So, why is Mexico City sinking?

**M035** Because it was built on a lake. Because the lake was drained. And because the city still drinks the water that holds up the ground beneath it.

**M036** Venice is a city fighting the water around it. Mexico City is a city that's running out of the water under it.

**M037** And the Angel of Independence keeps standing still, while the city slowly drops around it, one step at a time.
