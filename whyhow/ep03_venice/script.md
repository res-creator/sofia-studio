## COLD OPEN

**V001** This city should not exist. It stands in the middle of a salt-water lagoon. There's no solid ground under it. Just water, sand, and soft mud.

**V002** And yet, for more than a thousand years, it has held up heavy stone palaces, churches and bell towers. All of them, standing on mud.

**V003** The secret is right under your feet. Under these streets, there's a forest. Millions of tree trunks, pushed straight down into the mud, and they're still down there, holding up the city.

**V004** Wood that has been under water for hundreds of years. So why hasn't it rotted away? Why doesn't Venice sink? And here's the twist: it is sinking. Just not the way you think.

---

## CH1 — A city built to hide

**V005** To understand Venice, start with the question nobody asks: who builds a city in a swamp? People who are running away. [1]

**V006** In the fifth and sixth centuries, as the Roman Empire fell apart, armies swept into northern Italy. People from the mainland towns fled to the only place the armies couldn't follow: the lagoon. Low muddy islands, hidden channels, water too shallow for warships. [1]

**V007** Only locals knew where the safe channels were. To an invader, the lagoon was a maze. That made it the perfect place to hide, and later, the perfect place to trade. [1]

**V007B** It worked. For about eleven hundred years, Venice was an independent republic, ruled by an elected leader called the Doge. Its ships carried silk, spices, salt and glass between Europe and the East, and this city on mud became one of the richest places on Earth. [21]

**V007C** Its shipyard, the Arsenal, was the biggest industrial site in Europe. At its peak, it is said to have employed around sixteen thousand people, and in wartime it could turn out close to one ship a day, centuries before the factory assembly line. [21]

**V008** Today Venice is spread across more than a hundred small islands, split by canals and joined by around four hundred bridges. But every one of those islands had to be made strong enough to build on. [2]

---

## CH2 — The upside-down forest

**V009** Here's the problem. The top layer of the lagoon is soft mud. Put a stone house on it, and it slowly sinks in, like a boot in wet sand.

**V010** But a few meters down, there's something better: a layer of hard, compacted clay that the Venetians called caranto. The trick was to reach it. [3]

**V010B** There were no machines. Teams of workers lifted a heavy weight on ropes and dropped it onto the top of a pile, again and again, until the pile was driven deep into the ground. Then the next one. And the next.

**V011** So the builders took tree trunks, mostly larch, oak and alder, sharpened them, and hammered them straight down into the mud, one next to the other, as close as they could fit them. [3][4]

**V012** Packed that tightly, the trunks squeeze the mud between them until the whole block becomes hard. The ground under the building turns into something closer to solid rock. [3]

**V013** On top of the trunks went a flat wooden platform. On top of that, blocks of white Istrian stone, a stone that almost doesn't let water in. And only above the waterline, the brick walls. [3]

**V014** Look at almost any building on the canals, and you can see it: a white stone band at the bottom, where the water touches, and brick above it. That white line is the building's raincoat. [3]

**V015** And the numbers are crazy. The Rialto Bridge, finished in fifteen ninety-one, stands on about twelve thousand wooden piles. [5]

**V016** The church of Santa Maria della Salute, built after a plague, is said to stand on more than a million of them. [4]

**V017** All that wood had to come from somewhere. Whole forests in the mountains to the north were cut and floated down rivers to the lagoon. Venice is, quite literally, a forest turned upside down. [4]

---

## CH3 — Why doesn't the wood rot?

**V018** Now, the obvious question. Wood in water rots. Leave a wooden post in a lake, and in a few years it's soft and black. So why is Venice still standing?

**V018B** You can actually see the danger out in the lagoon. Those wooden posts sticking out of the water mark the safe channels for boats. Out there, in open water, they're worn down by the tides and chewed by tiny shipworms, and they have to be replaced again and again. [22]

**V019** Quick test. Myth or true: the piles under Venice never rot, because there's no oxygen in the mud? [6]

**V020** Partly true. Most of the fungi that destroy wood need oxygen. Deep in the wet clay, there's almost none, so the fungi can't survive. That's why the piles have lasted for centuries. [6]

**V021** But they're not frozen in time. Scientists from the University of Padua found that bacteria can still eat the wood, very slowly, even without oxygen. The outside of a pile softens, while the core stays strong. [6][7]

**V022** So why doesn't the city collapse? Deep in the mud, the shipworms can't reach the piles. And the water saves them twice. The mud around the piles holds them tight, and the water that fills the softened wood helps it keep its shape. The forest is still working. Just slowly getting older. [6]

## CH4 — A city without cars

**V022B** And every brick, every stone, every one of those piles, came here by boat. Because in the historic center of Venice, there are no cars. No buses. You're not even allowed to ride a bicycle.

**V022C** The buses here are boats, called vaporetti, stopping at floating platforms along the Grand Canal. Taxis are boats. The police are boats. [18]

**V022D** If you call an ambulance, a boat comes, siren and all, and races through the canals to the hospital. The fire brigade has boats too. Even the rubbish is collected by boat every morning and taken to another island. [18]

**V022E** Every shop, every restaurant and every hotel is supplied by water. Cargo boats bring in food, drinks, building materials, even washing machines. Then it's all moved by hand trolley, up and down the steps of the bridges. [18]

**V022F** Even the last journey is by water. The city's cemetery is an entire island, San Michele, and funerals arrive there by boat. [19]

**V022G** And the gondola? In the sixteenth century, Venice had around ten thousand of them. Today, there are only about four hundred, and almost all of them carry tourists. [20]

## CH5 — So is Venice sinking?

**V023** So the foundations hold. Then why do people keep saying Venice is sinking? Because it is. Just not because of the wood.

**V024** In the nineteen fifties and sixties, factories on the mainland, at Porto Marghera, pumped huge amounts of fresh water out of the ground below. The ground under the lagoon compressed, like a sponge being squeezed. [8]

**V025** In about twenty years, Venice dropped around twelve centimeters. That doesn't sound like much. For a city that sits barely above the water, it's enormous. [8]

**V026** In the early nineteen seventies, the pumping was stopped, and the fast sinking stopped with it. But the ground under Venice still settles a little, naturally, every year. [8][9]

**V027** And at the same time, the sea is rising. Put the two together, and over the last century and a half, the water around Venice has risen by about two and a half millimeters every year compared to the city. Slow, but it never stops. [9]

---

## CH6 — When the sea walks in

**V028** And that's how Venice gets its most famous problem: acqua alta. High water. [10]

**V029** In autumn and winter, a high tide, a strong wind from the south, and low air pressure can all line up. The Adriatic is pushed into the lagoon, and the water simply walks into the city. [10]

**V030** Venice even has its own alarm for it. When a dangerous tide is coming, sirens sound across the city, a few hours ahead. One tone means the water will reach a hundred and ten centimeters. Two tones, a hundred and twenty. Four tones means a hundred and forty or more. [11]

**V031** The lowest place in the city is St Mark's Square. The water starts coming up through the drains at around eighty centimeters, so the square floods first. That's why you see tourists walking on raised wooden walkways, or wading in rubber boots. [12]

**V031B** And it's getting more frequent. In the nineteen fifties, the water went above a hundred and ten centimeters less than twice a year on average. In the last decade, it's more than ten times a year. In twenty nineteen alone, it happened twenty-eight times. [23]

**V031C** Every time, salt water soaks into the walls. When it dries, the salt crystallizes inside the bricks and slowly breaks them apart. That's why so many Venetian walls look like they're peeling.

**V032** The worst flood on record came in November nineteen sixty-six: a hundred and ninety-four centimeters. [13]

**V033** Then, on the night of the twelfth of November, twenty nineteen, it almost happened again. A hundred and eighty-seven centimeters. Water poured into St Mark's Basilica. Boats were thrown onto the pavements. The city declared a state of emergency. [13]

---

## CH7 — Seventy-eight steel gates

**V034** For decades, Venice had a plan for exactly that night. It was called MOSE. And in twenty nineteen, it still wasn't working. [14]

**V035** Here's how it works. The lagoon is connected to the sea through three openings. On the seabed, in those openings, lie seventy-eight giant steel gates, hollow, and full of water. [14]

**V036** When a big tide is coming, air is pumped in. The water is pushed out, the gates float up, and in about half an hour, a wall of steel separates the lagoon from the sea. [14]

**V037** On October the third, twenty twenty, the gates were raised for the first time to stop a real tide. And for the first time in centuries, the sea outside rose, and the city stayed dry. [14]

**V038** By March twenty twenty-five, MOSE had been raised a hundred times. But it came very late, and very expensive: more than six billion euros, and every single raising costs hundreds of thousands more. [14][15]

**V038B** The gates only rise for big tides. Smaller floods still reach the lowest corners of the city. So in twenty twenty-two, St Mark's Basilica got its own protection: a wall of glass all around it, to keep out the water that MOSE lets in. [12]

**V039** And there's a catch. Every time the gates close, the lagoon is cut off from the sea. Ships can't get in, and the water can't refresh itself. The more the sea rises, the more often the gates will have to close. [14]

---

## CH8 — The other flood

**V040** But the water isn't the only thing flooding Venice.

**V041** Every year, somewhere between twenty-five and thirty million visitors come to Venice. On the busiest days, the narrow streets jam like a stadium exit. [16]

**V042** Meanwhile, the people who actually live here are leaving. In nineteen fifty-one, the historic center had about a hundred and seventy-five thousand residents. In twenty twenty-two, it fell below fifty thousand for the first time. [17]

**V043** Rents went up, apartments turned into holiday rentals, and shops for residents became shops for tourists. Locals moved to the mainland. [17]

**V044** So in twenty twenty-four, Venice became the first city in the world to charge day visitors just to walk in: five euros on the busiest days, ten if you book at the last minute. [16]

## ENDING

**V045** So, why doesn't Venice sink?

**V046** Because under the palaces, there's a forest. Millions of trunks, driven into the clay by hand, packed so tight they turned mud into rock. And they're still down there, in the dark, doing their job after centuries.

**V047** The real threat to Venice isn't under the city. It's all around it: a sea that keeps rising, and a city that keeps emptying.

**V048** Venice was built by people running from danger. For more than a thousand years, it has found a clever way to survive. The question now is whether it can find one more.
