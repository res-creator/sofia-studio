# EP07 — Who Was the Richest Person in History?

Format: How It's Run, English, voice am_puck speed 1.08 (~190 wpm), target ~11–12 min. Mostly real footage and archive photos of the places where each fortune was made (Texas, Medellín, Cleveland, Pittsburgh, Detroit, Hyderabad, Augsburg, Timbuktu, Cairo, Rome); cut every 2–4 s; grid graphics as short inserts (counters, comparison bars, maps).
Numbers in [brackets] point to SOURCES.md. Our yardstick through the film: a fortune as a share of its country's whole economy.

---

## COLD OPEN

**R001** Imagine earning an average American salary, and saving every single dollar. No rent, no food, nothing. To save as much as the richest man alive, you would have to start more than fifteen million years ago. [1][2]

**R002** In twenty twenty-six, Elon Musk became the first person ever whose fortune was estimated at around one trillion dollars. [1]

**R003** But was he the richest person who ever lived? Not so fast. There were people who owned entire industries, entire diamond mines, even an entire country.

**R004** So let's travel back through two thousand years of money, from Texas to ancient Rome, and find out who was really the richest person in history.

---

## CH1 — The trillion-dollar man

**R005** First, a problem. How do you even compare fortunes across centuries?

**R006** A dollar in nineteen hundred bought far more than a dollar today. So in this video, we'll use one simple measuring stick: how big was the fortune compared with the whole economy of its country? [3]

**R007** Now, Musk. Most of his money isn't in a bank. It's shares: in Tesla, the electric car company, and in SpaceX, the rocket company that went public in twenty twenty-six. [1]

**R008** When those shares jump, his fortune jumps. In one day in October twenty twenty-six, it grew by about sixty billion dollars. That's more than most countries produce in a year. [1]

**R009** The American economy is worth around thirty trillion dollars a year. So a trillion dollars is about three percent of it. Remember that number. Three percent. [1][4]

---

## CH2 — The cash that rats ate

**R010** Not every fortune lives on a stock exchange. In the nineteen eighties, one of the richest men on the planet kept much of his money in cash, hidden in walls, farms and fields around the city of Medellín, in Colombia.

**R011** Pablo Escobar ran a cocaine empire. For several years, Forbes magazine put him on its list of the world's billionaires. [5]

**R012** He had so much cash, it became a problem. According to his brother, who kept the books, the cartel spent around two and a half thousand dollars a month just on rubber bands to hold the bundles together. [6]

**R013** And every year, they wrote off about ten percent of the money, because rats chewed through it, or water ruined it in its hiding places. [6]

**R014** But this kind of wealth came with a price: violence, thousands of victims, and a life on the run. Escobar was killed in nineteen ninety-three, on a rooftop in Medellín. His fortune was huge, but it was never close to the real giants of history. [5]

---

## CH3 — The king of oil

**R015** For a real giant, we go back to Cleveland, Ohio, in the eighteen sixties. Oil had just been discovered in Pennsylvania, and a young bookkeeper named John D. Rockefeller saw that the real money wasn't in drilling. It was in refining. [7]

**R016** His company, Standard Oil, bought up its rivals one by one, struck secret deals with the railroads, and cut costs to the bone. At its peak, it controlled about ninety percent of the oil refined in the United States. [7]

**R017** In nineteen eleven, the Supreme Court ordered Standard Oil to be broken up into thirty-four separate companies. Some of them still exist, as Exxon, Mobil and Chevron. [7]

**R018** But here's the twist. The breakup made Rockefeller even richer. The shares of the new companies soared, and he owned a big piece of every one of them. [7]

**R019** By nineteen thirteen, his fortune was around nine hundred million dollars, at a time when the whole American economy was worth about thirty-nine billion. That's roughly two to three percent. [3][7]

**R020** For more than a hundred years, that made Rockefeller the benchmark: the richest American who ever lived. Today, measured the same way, Musk is right next to him, and possibly ahead. [1][3]

---

## CH4 — Steel and cars

**R021** Rockefeller wasn't alone. In Pittsburgh, a Scottish immigrant who had started work as a child in a cotton mill built the biggest steel business in America. His name was Andrew Carnegie. [8]

**R022** In nineteen-oh-one, he sold his steel company to the banker J.P. Morgan for four hundred and eighty million dollars. Morgan reportedly shook his hand and said: congratulations, Mr. Carnegie, you are now the richest man in the world. [8]

**R023** Then Carnegie did something unusual. He gave it away. He believed that a man who dies rich dies disgraced. He paid for more than two and a half thousand public libraries around the world. [8]

**R024** A few hundred miles away, in Detroit, Henry Ford had a different idea. Make a car so cheap that ordinary workers could buy one. [9]

**R025** In nineteen thirteen, he introduced the moving assembly line. Building a Model T chassis dropped from more than twelve hours to about an hour and a half. The price of the car kept falling, from eight hundred and fifty dollars to around two hundred and sixty. [9]

**R026** Ford then doubled his workers' pay to five dollars a day, so they could afford the cars they built. His family owned the entire company, and he became one of the richest men in America. [9]

---

## CH5 — The man with a diamond paperweight

**R027** Now let's leave America. In nineteen thirty-seven, Time magazine put a small, thin man on its cover, and called him the richest man in the world. [10]

**R028** He was Mir Osman Ali Khan, the Nizam of Hyderabad, ruler of a kingdom in southern India about the size of Britain. [10]

**R029** His land sat near the famous Golconda mines, where some of the most famous diamonds in history were found. In the nineteen forties, his fortune was estimated at around two billion dollars, about two percent of the American economy at the time. [10]

**R030** And yet he was famous for being stingy. He wore old clothes and smoked cheap cigarettes. On his desk, he used a diamond as a paperweight: the Jacob Diamond, a stone of about a hundred and eighty-five carats. [10]

**R031** After India became independent, his kingdom was absorbed into the new country. His jewels now belong to the Indian state and are kept in a central bank vault. [10]

---

## CH6 — The banker who bought an emperor

**R032** Now we go back five hundred years, to the city of Augsburg, in southern Germany. Here lived a merchant who may have been the most powerful banker in history: Jakob Fugger. [11]

**R033** He traded cloth and spices, then took over silver and copper mines across Central Europe. And he lent money to kings. [11]

**R034** In fifteen nineteen, the throne of the Holy Roman Empire was up for election. The voters were seven princes, and they expected to be paid. [11]

**R035** The young Charles of Habsburg needed hundreds of thousands of gold coins to win. Most of them came from Fugger. Charles won, and became Emperor Charles the Fifth. [11]

**R036** A few years later, when the emperor was slow to pay him back, Fugger sent him a letter. It said, more or less: it is well known that without me, Your Majesty would not have the imperial crown. [11]

**R037** Fugger also left something behind that still works today. In fifteen sixteen, he built the Fuggerei, a little walled town for poor families in Augsburg. It's the oldest social housing complex in the world that is still in use. [12]

**R038** The rent hasn't changed in five hundred years. It is still the equivalent of one old Rhine guilder a year. Today that's eighty-eight euro cents. Plus three prayers a day for the Fugger family. [12]

---

## CH7 — The king who crashed the price of gold

**R039** Now we go deeper, seven hundred years back, to West Africa. In the thirteen hundreds, the Mali Empire controlled some of the richest gold fields in the known world, and the trade routes across the Sahara. [13]

**R040** Its ruler was Mansa Musa. And in thirteen twenty-four, he set off on a pilgrimage to Mecca, with a caravan that people talked about for centuries. Thousands of people, camels, and huge amounts of gold. [13]

**R041** When he passed through Cairo, he gave away and spent so much gold that its value in the city fell. The historian al-Umari, who wrote about Cairo a few years later, said the price still hadn't recovered by his time. [13]

**R042** Back home, Mansa Musa used his wealth to build. Under his rule, Timbuktu grew into a famous center of trade and learning, with mosques made of mud that still stand today. [13]

**R043** In thirteen seventy-five, a European mapmaker drew the Catalan Atlas, and right in the middle of Africa, he painted a king on a throne, holding a golden nugget. That king is Mansa Musa. [13]

**R044** How rich was he? Nobody knows. There are no accounts and no records. Modern lists sometimes call him the richest person ever, but any number you see is a guess.

---

## CH8 — The man who owned Egypt

**R045** And now our last stop: Rome, two thousand years ago.

**R046** After decades of civil war, one man ended up in control of the whole Roman world: Octavian, who took the name Augustus, and became the first Roman emperor. [14]

**R047** After he defeated Cleopatra, Egypt, the breadbasket of the ancient world, wasn't simply added to the empire like other provinces. It was run by his own governor, and senators weren't even allowed to visit without his permission. [14]

**R048** At the end of his life, Augustus wrote a list of his achievements, and it was carved in bronze outside his tomb. In it, he boasts about how much he paid from his own fortune: for the army, for the grain supply, and to rescue the public treasury itself. [15]

**R049** When the public treasury ran short, Augustus topped it up from his own pocket. Some modern estimates claim his fortune was equal to a fifth of the empire's whole economy. Those numbers are rough guesses, but the point is clear: for a while, his money and the empire's money were almost the same thing. [14][15]

---

## ENDING

**R050** So, who was the richest person in history?

**R051** If you only count dollars, it's Elon Musk. Nobody has ever had a fortune of a trillion.

**R052** If you compare fortunes with the economy of their time, Musk and Rockefeller are almost neck and neck, at around three percent of the American economy. [3]

**R053** But if you count power, what money could actually buy, then Mansa Musa and Augustus are in a league of their own. One could change the price of gold in a foreign city. The other could rescue the treasury of an empire.

**R054** And Jakob Fugger? He did something none of the others did. He bought an emperor.
