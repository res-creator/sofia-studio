[1:42] NARRATOR: The volcano has not erupted since eighteen fifty-seven. Now the earthquakes don't stop. Within a week, about thirty a day are magnitude three or larger.

[1:53] NARRATOR: On March twenty-seventh, just after noon, a loud boom is heard around the mountain. A steam explosion has blasted a crater through the ice at the summit.

[2:03] NARRATOR: It is the first eruption in the lower forty-eight states since Lassen Peak, more than sixty years earlier.

[2:10] NARRATOR: The explosions are driven by steam, not by fresh magma reaching the surface. But they keep coming, and within a week the crater is more than a thousand feet across.

[2:21] NARRATOR: Geologists from the U.S. Geological Survey set up a base in Vancouver, Washington. One of them, Dwight Crandell, tells a reporter what worries him.

[2:30] [CARD] “Mount St. Helens has done so many different things in the past that hardly anything would be a surprise… The only thing it hasn’t done is blow itself apart.” — Dwight Crandell, USGS (Seattle Times, April 6, 1980; quoted in USGS PP 1249)

[2:37] NARRATOR: Crandell knew this volcano better than almost anyone. Two years earlier, he and Donal Mullineaux had published a hazard report on Mount St. Helens.

[2:46] NARRATOR: It called it an especially dangerous volcano, and it made a forecast. An eruption was more likely within the next hundred years... and perhaps even before the end of this century.

[2:59] NARRATOR: The same page is just as clear about what could not be known.

[3:03] [DOCUMENT] USGS Bulletin 1383-C (1978), p. C25: “Because of the variable behavior of the volcano, we cannot be sure whether the next eruption will produce lava flows, pyroclastic flows, tephra, or volcanic domes…”

[3:08] NARRATOR: The danger was known. The form it would take was not. And this spring, the clue was not at the summit. It was on the north side.

[3:17] DON SWANSON (USGS): All the deformation on the mountain was, was local to the mountain, and even more local than that was on the north side of the mountain only.

[3:26] NARRATOR: From the first day of the eruption, observers had noticed an uplift high on the north flank. By mid-April, it was impossible to miss.

[3:35] DAN MILLER (USGS): In the nearly two-month period before May 18, what was essentially happening at Mount St. Helens was that magma or molten material was moving up from some deep reservoir beneath the mountain up into the volcano itself and it began to grow, um, or form what we call a dome or a cryptodome inside the volcano. And that inflating body of magma or molten material actually broke the north side of the volcano and began to cause the north side of the volcano to expand out toward the north.

[4:04] NARRATOR: To follow it, the scientists put survey targets on the bulge and measured them every few days: angles by theodolite, distances by laser, from stations on the ridges to the north. One of those stations was Coldwater Two.

[4:18] NARRATOR: The measurements told the same story day after day. The north flank was moving outward about five feet a day, at an almost steady rate.

[4:28] NARRATOR: A landslide from that slope was recognized as a real hazard, one that could set off an eruption. What the scientists did not foresee, as they later wrote, was the scale of what would follow.

[4:40] NARRATOR: There were also decisions to make about people. On April thirtieth, Governor Dixy Lee Ray closed a Red Zone around the peak, three to eight miles out, and a wider Blue Zone, where loggers and landowners could enter with permits during the day.

[4:55] NARRATOR: Not everyone left. At Spirit Lake, five miles north of the summit, eighty-three-year-old Harry Truman refused to leave the lodge he had run for more than fifty years.

[5:06] NARRATOR: Cabin owners pressed to get their belongings out. On Saturday, May seventeenth, deputies escorted about fifty carloads of them into the Red Zone. A second caravan was set for ten o'clock the next morning.

[5:20] NARRATOR: That afternoon, David Johnston took over the post at Coldwater Two from fellow geologist Harry Glicken. It stood five point seven miles from the summit.

[5:30] NARRATOR: Eight miles northwest, at Coldwater One, photographer Reid Blackburn of The Columbian stayed overnight beside a camera station. On the next ridge north of Johnston, a volunteer radio operator, Gerry Martin, was also keeping watch.

[5:45] NARRATOR: At six fifty-three on Sunday morning, Johnston radioed in his last survey readings. Nothing in the data looked unusual.

[5:53] NARRATOR: At eight thirty-two and eleven seconds, a magnitude five-point-one earthquake struck beneath the volcano.

[6:00] NARRATOR: Johnston's call to Vancouver was picked up on a private citizen's radio. A second message was too garbled to understand. Then, nothing.

[6:10] NARRATOR: What happened in the next minutes has been reconstructed in detail, from photographs taken by survivors, and from what the eruption left behind.

[6:19] NARRATOR: Within about twenty seconds of the earthquake, the bulge and the summit above it broke loose and slid north, in a series of enormous blocks.

[6:28] NARRATOR: It was the largest landslide in recorded history: about two-thirds of a cubic mile of rock.

[6:35] NARRATOR: And as it moved, it stripped away the rock that had been holding in the magma inside the north flank.

[6:41] DAN MILLER (USGS): On the morning of May 18, what actually happened, a landslide basically uncorked this pressurized body of magma and allowed it to, um, explode or expand out towards the north very rapidly. This is what we call the lateral blast.

[6:55] NARRATOR: The blast tore out of the side of the mountain toward the north, and quickly overtook the avalanche. Within about six miles, almost nothing was left standing.

[7:05] NARRATOR: Farther out, entire forests were flattened, the trees all pointing the way the blast had moved. At its outer edge, the trees stayed upright, but were scorched dead.

[7:16] DAN MILLER (USGS): The directed blast was really the most destructive event that occurred on the morning of May 18. It completely destroyed an area of 230 square miles in the matter of, um, somewhere between five and, and nine minutes.

[7:42] NARRATOR: Then the eruption turned upward. Within fifteen minutes, a column of ash rose about fifteen miles into the sky. It kept erupting for nine hours.

[7:54] NARRATOR: When it ended, the summit was thirteen hundred feet lower. In its place was a crater, open to the north... the direction the mountain had been bulging for weeks.
