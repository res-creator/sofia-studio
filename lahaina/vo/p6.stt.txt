Herman Andaya was not on Maui that afternoon. He was at a conference on Oahu. At four oh three, as the fire was moving toward town, he texted, "Should I come home?" Hawaii has one of the largest outdoor siren systems in the world. On Maui alone, there are eighty sirens. On August eighth, not one of them was sounded. A week later, a reporter asked Andaya if he regretted that decision. He answered, "I do not." He explained that people are trained to seek higher ground when a siren sounds. "We just don't use sirens for fires," he said. "Had we sounded the siren that night," he said, "we were afraid that people would have gone mauka, toward the mountain, and into the fire." The next day, he resigned, citing health reasons. The investigation that followed found the full story was worse. Nobody had even discussed using the sirens. They had never been used for a wildfire, not even in twenty eighteen. And of the five sirens around Lahaina, only two were listed as working. They relied on a cell network that was already failing. Investigators concluded that a siren might not have told people what to do, but it might have given them one more warning.
