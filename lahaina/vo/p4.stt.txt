This time, nothing could stop it. The fire jumped the Lahaina Bypass. Around three thirty, the first homes on Lahainaluna Road caught fire. At three thirty, Andaya, the head of Maui's emergency agency, sent a text: "Closing Lahaina Bypass Road. Fire jump over to the roadway." Police radio logs capture the speed of it. At three twenty-three, an officer reports blocking the bypass into Lahaina. At three fifty-two, Front Street at a standstill. The wind had already knocked out cell towers. Power was out. Many people had no idea how close the fire was. Alerts did go out, a road closure. Then at four ten, an evacuation order for one neighborhood at the top of town. But when investigators later interviewed seventy-one residents, eighty-seven percent said they never received a single text alert. At four twenty-four, someone reported fire near the banyan tree. That is more than a mile from where the morning fire began. At the same minute, the first death was reported on Mill Street. At four forty-six, the fire crossed the highway into the heart of Lahaina. Officers were breaking open locked gates so residents could escape. Embers were flying ahead of the flames, lighting roofs blocks away, and the roads out of town were jammed. A fallen utility pole blocked one direction of the highway. Traffic was turned away in the other. Survivors describe police cars and utility crews blocking roads. The police chief later said officers never stopped anyone from leaving. Accounts still conflict, but many survivors agree on one thing: the people who drove around the barricades lived. Another said the gridlock would have left us there when the firestorm came. One survivor told reporters, "We literally had minutes, and one wrong turn, we would all be dead right now."
