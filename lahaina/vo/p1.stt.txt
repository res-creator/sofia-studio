To understand how this happened, you have to understand Lahaina. For a time in the 1800s, this small town on the west coast of Maui was the royal capital of the Kingdom of Hawaii. King Kamehameha III lived here on a tiny island in the middle of a spring-fed pond called Moku'inia. Lahaina was a place of water. Streams ran down from the mountains through taro fields and fish ponds to the sea. Then came the whaling ships, and after them, sugar. Plantations diverted the mountain streams to water their cane. The royal pond dried up. In 1919, it was filled in and turned into a baseball field. By the 21st century, Lahaina was one of the driest places in Hawaii, with about 15 inches of rain a year. Front Street ran along the ocean, lined with wooden buildings from the whaling days. In the town square stood a banyan tree, planted in 1873, that had grown to cover almost an acre, and on the hills above the town, something else was growing. When the plantations closed, the fields were left empty. African grasses brought in to feed cattle took over: guinea grass, buffel grass, molasses grass. Guinea grass can grow six inches in a single day. When it dries out, it burns with flames up to 20 feet high. By 2023, Lahaina was a historic wooden town surrounded on three sides by fuel.
