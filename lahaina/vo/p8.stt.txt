So who was responsible? The answer that came back was not one person. It was a system. Hawaiian Electric had no program to shut off power before dangerous winds. Its chief executive told Congress such shutoffs were not an appropriate fit for Hawaii. In October twenty twenty-four, the official cause report ruled the fire accidental. Broken, re-energized power lines had started the morning fire. The afternoon fire was that same fire rekindled. The lead federal agent said of the firefighters, "I do believe they did everything possible." Hawaiian Electric said it deeply regretted that its operations contributed to the fire that ignited in the morning. The state's independent investigation went further. It listed eighty-four findings and concluded that no single factor caused the disaster. The attorney general called it a wake-up call for the state and county governments. Warnings that were never studied, a fire that came back after the crews had gone, alerts that never arrived, sirens nobody considered, roads that clogged, hydrants that ran dry. As the head of the institute that led the investigation put it, "The conditions that made this tragedy possible were years in the making." In twenty twenty-four, seven defendants, including the state, Maui County, and Hawaiian Electric, agreed to a settlement of more than four billion dollars. Hawaiian Electric now has a program to cut the power when dangerous winds arrive. It started in July twenty twenty-four. The company calls it a last line of defense.
